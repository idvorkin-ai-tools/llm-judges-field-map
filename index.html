LLM Judges, mapped

An LLM judge reads a model's output and grades it. This course is about whether to believe the grade: what the judge is told good means, how it is asked, whether judges agree, whether they track the truth, how they go wrong, and how to check one before trusting it.

Like everything in ML, nothing here is new. People have graded without an answer key for centuries. Interviewers decide who to hire, managers rate a half-year of work, wine judges award medals, radiologists decide who to call back, umpires call strikes. Each of those fields has already found how judges disagree, drift and flatter, and has statistics for it: Cohen's kappa came from psychology, sensitivity, specificity and Youden's J from diagnostic medicine, the halo effect from officers rating soldiers. An LLM judge inherits all of it. The menu at the top switches every example on the site between those five worlds; the concepts and the numbers stay the same.

ARC 0

Nothing here is new

Why anyone uses a judge, why it is a measuring instrument, and the two questions every instrument must answer. After this arc you can separate reliability from validity and say where the truth for a task comes from.

01
Nothing here is new
why judges · reliability and validity · where truth comes from
The judge as the newest member of an old profession: anonymous exam scripts, the halo effect, reader variability, kappa and replicate pours all came first. Reliability against validity, and a single axis from no outside truth (wine quality) to measured truth (pitch calls) with LLM tasks placed on it.
ARC 1

What are we asking?

Before measuring a judge, fix what it is told and how it is asked. After this arc you can write a policy a stranger would apply the same way, and pick between scoring, comparing and checking against a reference.

02
The policy: what good means
rubrics · anchors · binary vs scales · criteria drift
A judge is only as good as the written policy it applies. What a policy contains, why anchored and binary criteria beat vague scales, why people must read real outputs before anything is automated, and how disagreement gets fixed in the policy rather than in the judge.
03
Three ways to ask: score one, compare two, check against a reference
pointwise · pairwise · reference-guided
Absolute scores and how judges use scales differently; pairwise preferences and how they become a ranking; reference answers and how they anchor. When to use which.
ARC 2

Measuring the judge

Reliability, then validity. After this arc you can compute and read kappa, alpha, sensitivity, specificity, Youden's J and an ROC curve, and say what each one hides.

04
Do the judges agree?
percent agreement · Cohen's κ · Fleiss' κ · Krippendorff's α
Why 75% agreement can be a kappa of 0.49 and 92% a kappa of 0.29; three or more judges; confidence intervals; and the bar that matters: a judge should agree with people about as well as people agree with each other.
05
Judge versus truth: the confusion matrix, Youden's J and ROC
sensitivity · specificity · J · precision · base rates · ROC/AUC
The casebook judge at sensitivity 0.80 and specificity 0.85 (J = 0.65); why its precision falls from 0.70 to 0.22 when the thing it looks for gets rare; thresholds, the ROC curve and choosing a cut-off by cost.
ARC 3

How judges go wrong

The failure modes, each with its LLM form, its older human form and its fix. After this arc you can name the bias behind a suspicious score and the check that exposes it.

06
How judges go wrong I: what they are shown
position · order · verbosity · anchoring · contrast
Biases from presentation: the answer shown first wins, longer looks better, a prior score anchors the next, and the last few decisions shape the next one.
07
How judges go wrong II: who is judging
self-preference · leniency · halo · drift · Goodhart
Biases from the judge and its incentives: favouring its own kind, a shifted or squashed scale, one trait colouring the rest, fatigue and silent version changes, and what happens when people optimise against the judge.
ARC 4

Using a judge

From theory to a checklist. After this arc you can validate a judge on your own task, put error bars on it, and say when its grade should only be a flag.

08
Calibrating and validating a judge
gold sets · train/dev/test · bias checks · error bars · routing · audits
The practical loop: humans label first, measure their agreement, treat the judge as a classifier, run the bias checks, report intervals, route the uncertain middle to people, and keep auditing.
09
When not to trust a judge
no agreement · self-grading · Goodhart · rare events · single items · injection
The cases where a grade should not be used or should only start a second look, and the remedies older fields use: second opinions, panels, appeals and calibration committees.
