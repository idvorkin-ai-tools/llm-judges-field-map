Do the judges agree?

Before asking whether a judge is right, ask whether two judges, or one judge twice, give the same answer, and how much of that sameness chance alone would buy.
TL;DR — Percent agreement is the number everyone reports and the one that flatters most: two raters who both usually say NO will match even when neither is paying attention. Cohen's kappa takes out the agreement chance would give; on the casebook grid, 75% agreement becomes kappa 0.49. When YES is rare, kappa can collapse even at 92% agreement, which is exactly the case of a judge hunting rare failures. Fleiss' kappa and Krippendorff's alpha extend the idea to more raters, missing labels and ordered scales. At 100 items all of them have wide error bars. The bar for an LLM judge is not 100%: it is agreeing with people about as well as people agree with each other, measured the same way on the same items.

Reliability is the cheap half of trusting a judge. It needs no truth, only a second opinion: another rater, or the same rater again. So it is the first thing to measure, and the first number people get wrong, because the obvious question, “how often did they match?”, is not the one it seems to be.

In an LLM evaluation the two raters are two human labelers, or one labeler and the LLM judge. The numbers are the same in every domain.

Two interviewers, one panel

Ana and Ben interview the same 100 candidates for the same role, each writing “hire” or “no hire” before the debrief, so neither sees the other's call. The company wants to know whether its interview process measures the candidate or the interviewer.

Two managers, one calibration

Carla and Dev each read the same 100 employees' half-year packets and mark “exceeds” or “meets” before the calibration meeting. If two informed managers cannot agree on paper, the meeting is negotiating, not measuring.

Two judges, one flight

Elodie and Femi taste the same 100 wines, poured blind, and each marks “medal” or “no medal” before the panel talks. Since nothing outside the panel says which wines deserved a medal, their agreement is most of what the competition can check.

Double reading

Dr Gale and Dr Hart each read the same 100 screening mammograms and decide “recall” or “clear”, without seeing the other's report. Several screening programmes double-read routinely; this is the same arrangement used as a measurement.

Two umpires, one video

Ivo and June each call the same 100 taken pitches from video, “strike” or “ball”, without hearing the other's call. In a live game only one umpire calls a pitch, so this is a study design, not normal practice.

Percent agreement flatters

The grid counts every item by the pair of verdicts it got. The diagonal is agreement: both raters called 45 items YES and 30 NO, so they agree on 75 of 100. That is percent agreement, or observed agreement, po = 0.75. It is honest and easy to explain, which is why almost every judge report uses it.

The trouble is what 75% is compared against. On a YES/NO label, two raters flipping coins agree half the time. Two raters who both lean NO agree more often than that without looking at the item. So 75% might be impressive or barely better than guessing, and the number alone cannot say which. Igor's notes put it in one line: percent agreement flatters.

Percent agreement also counts both kinds of agreement the same. In an LLM eval the interesting cases are usually the YES ones, such as a response that breaks the policy. Two raters can score high on the easy NO pile while disagreeing on most of the cases that matter. Keep the whole grid, not just its diagonal. Subtracting chance: Cohen's kappa

Jacob Cohen's fix (Cohen 1960), first used for psychologists coding the same behaviour, asks how often the raters would match if each kept their own habit but answered at random. Rater A says YES on 60 of 100 items, rater B on 55. If they decided independently, both would say YES on 0.60 × 0.55 = 33% of items and both NO on 0.40 × 0.45 = 18%. Chance agreement is pe = 0.51.

Worked · casebook two raters
κ = (po − pe) / (1 − pe) = (0.75 − 0.51) / (1 − 0.51) = 0.24 / 0.49 = 0.49

Kappa is 1 when raters agree on everything, 0 when they agree exactly as often as chance predicts, and negative when they agree less. 0.49 means they used about half the room above chance. Scott's pi, which builds chance from the two raters' pooled YES rate, gives 0.489 here; Krippendorff's alpha gives 0.491. On a clean two-rater grid they nearly coincide. Derivation and standard error are in the appendix.

pe depends only on the edge totals, each rater's YES rate. That is the point: kappa rewards raters for agreeing on which items get YES, not for sharing a habit of saying YES. Two lenient raters get no credit for being lenient together.

For an LLM judge, Cohen's kappa is the right first number when comparing the judge's YES/NO verdicts with one human's labels, or two humans with each other. Igor's evals recipe uses it and its relatives for both checks. Report it next to percent agreement and the grid, not instead of them.

The kappa paradox and rare failures

Now change only how common YES is. The same two raters judge a rare label: each says YES on 6 of 100 items. They agree on 92, 2 YES and 90 NO. Chance agreement is 0.06 × 0.06 + 0.94 × 0.94 = 0.887, so almost all of that 92% came free. Kappa is (0.92 − 0.887) / (1 − 0.887) = 0.29.

Feinstein and Cicchetti called this “high agreement but low kappa”, the kappa paradox (Feinstein and Cicchetti 1990). It is a paradox only if kappa is read as a grade. Read as a question, it is right: on the only items where anything happened, the raters' YES calls overlap on 2 of the 10 items either one flagged. A screening judge with that record would miss most of what its partner caught.

Rare in hiring

For a senior role where the bar is high, nearly every candidate is a “no hire”. Ana and Ben can agree 92% of the time and still disagree on most of the few people they would actually hire. That is the number the hiring manager cares about.

Rare in reviews

If “exceeds” is reserved for a small share of employees, Carla and Dev agree on the large “meets” group by default. The question that matters for pay and promotion is whether they pick the same few, and kappa says they mostly do not.

Rare at the competition

When only a handful of wines in a flight earn a medal, Elodie and Femi agree on the many “no medal” glasses easily. Whether their medal lists overlap is the real test, and in this grid they share 2 of 10 flagged wines.

Rare in screening

Most screening mammograms are normal, so Dr Gale and Dr Hart agree on the “clear” films almost automatically. Screening is about the rare abnormal ones, and those are where their calls part.

Rare behind the plate

Suppose the sample is mostly pitches thrown well off the plate, so a called strike is rare. Ivo and June agree on the obvious balls without effort; the few strikes are where their calls part.

This is the normal case for LLM judges. Safety violations, made-up citations and broken tool calls are rare by design, and the judge exists to find them. Three habits follow. Report agreement on YES cases separately from agreement on NO cases, so a big easy pile cannot hide a small hard one. Add likely failures to the validation set so the YES class has enough items to measure; unit 05 covers what that does to precision. And when kappa is low but percent agreement high, check the prevalence before blaming the raters. The arithmetic is in the appendix.

Three or more judges, and ordered scales

Cohen's kappa is for exactly two raters. Panels are common, and so are setups where many raters each label a subset. Joseph Fleiss extended kappa to any fixed number of raters per item, giving Fleiss' kappa (Fleiss 1971). The idea is the same: on each item, count the share of rater pairs that agree, average over items, and compare with the pair agreement chance would give from the overall YES rate. Because it pools the raters' YES rates, it extends Scott's pi rather than Cohen's kappa, despite the name.

Worked · casebook three judges

100 items, three judges each. 35 items got three YES votes, 15 got two, 12 got one, 38 got none. On a unanimous item all three pairs agree; on a 2–1 split, one pair of three does. Mean pair agreement is (73 × 1 + 27 × 1/3) / 100 = 0.82. The overall YES rate is 0.49, so chance pair agreement is 0.49² + 0.51² = 0.50.

Fleiss' κ = (0.82 − 0.50) / (1 − 0.50) = 0.64

Krippendorff's alpha on the same data is 0.641. Derivation in the appendix.

The interview panel

A loop of three interviewers each writes a verdict before the debrief. On 73 of 100 candidates the loop is unanimous; the 27 split candidates are where the debrief earns its time, and where a single interviewer's habit decides the outcome if nobody looks.

The calibration committee

Three managers rate every packet in a group. The unanimous 73 can go through quickly; the 27 splits are what the calibration meeting should spend its hour on.

The three-judge panel

A typical competition panel seats several judges per flight. Unanimous wines are easy; the 27 split wines are where the panel chair or a discussion settles the medal.

Triple reading

Some programmes send discordant double reads to a third reader for arbitration. With three readers on every film, 73 of 100 get the same call from all; the 27 split films are the ones arbitration exists for.

The umpire crew on review

Three umpires call each taken pitch from video. Unanimous on 73 pitches; the 27 split pitches are the edges of the zone, which is also where tracking cameras matter most.

For LLM judges the three-rater case shows up two ways. Several humans label the same gold items, and Fleiss' kappa says whether the policy is clear enough to act as truth. Or several judge calls (different models, or one model sampled several times) vote on each item, and the split items go to a person.

Krippendorff's alpha. Krippendorff's alpha comes from content analysis, where coders often label different subsets and data has gaps. It measures disagreement: one minus the disagreement observed over the disagreement chance would give. So one formula handles any number of raters, missing labels, and any kind of scale (categories, ranks or numbers), with a distance function to match (Hayes and Krippendorff 2007). On the casebook it tracks the kappas: 0.491 for the two raters, 0.641 for the three judges.

Alpha also has the most-quoted threshold in the field. Krippendorff's guidance, as summarised on Wikipedia from his 2004 textbook: rely on data with α ≥ 0.800, use 0.667 to 0.800 only for tentative conclusions, and discard data below 0.667, with the right minimum depending on what a wrong conclusion would cost. By that bar, none of the casebook raters produce labels anyone should treat as truth yet. Details in the appendix.

Weighted kappa. Many judges give a 1–5 score, not YES/NO. Plain kappa treats a 4 against a 5 as the same miss as a 1 against a 5. Weighted kappa (Cohen 1968) gives partial credit by distance, so near misses cost little and far misses a lot. Alpha with a rank or number distance does the same job. The mammography study below reports this kind of number. Look at the full score grid too: one number hides whether disagreements are scattered or one rater runs a point high (appendix).

Benchmark words and error bars

Every kappa eventually meets a sentence like “0.49 is moderate”. The words come from Landis and Koch: slight (0–0.20), fair (0.21–0.40), moderate (0.41–0.60), substantial (0.61–0.80) and almost perfect (0.81–1) (Landis and Koch 1977). The bands are a convenience, not a finding: they gave no evidence for the cut points, and Fleiss later proposed different, equally arbitrary ones (Cohen's kappa, Wikipedia). Use the words in conversation and the number, with its interval, for decisions.

The interval is the bigger problem. On 100 items, kappa 0.49 has an approximate 95% confidence interval of 0.32 to 0.66; a bootstrap gives 0.31 to 0.66. Percent agreement of 75/100 has a Wilson interval of 0.66 to 0.83. In Landis and Koch's words the raters are somewhere between fair and substantial: 100 items cannot tell. A second labelling run at 0.40 or 0.60 is not news. Halving the interval takes about four times as many items, so a judge checked on 50 hand-labelled examples should be reported with its interval, not as one figure (appendix; Igor's note on error bars).

The rare-label case is worse. In the paradox grid only 10 items involve a YES from anyone, so the statistic rests on 10 items, whatever the total says.

Agreeing with itself

Inter-rater agreement compares two judges. Intra-rater agreement compares a judge with itself: the same item, the same rater, twice, without the rater knowing it is a repeat. It is the cheapest reliability check, and it caps everything else: a judge cannot agree with anyone more consistently than it agrees with itself.

The same candidate, twice

An interviewer rarely sees the same candidate twice without remembering them, so this is hard to test live. Recorded interviews make it possible: the same interviewer scores the same recording weeks apart, and any change is the instrument, not the candidate.

The same packet, twice

A manager who re-reads the same packet in a different week, or after a different stack of packets, can land on a different rating. Scoring anonymised packets twice is the review world's version of a repeat measurement.

The same wine, three times

Robert Hodgson had the California State Fair pour some wines three times, from the same bottle, within a judge's flight of 30. Over four years only about 10% of judges kept a wine's three scores within one medal group, and another 10% on occasion scored the same wine anywhere from Bronze to Gold (Hodgson 2008).

The same film, months later

A reader study can slip previously read films back into a radiologist's queue once enough time has passed that the reader is unlikely to recall them. Any change in the call is the reader, since the film did not change.

The same pitch, on replay

An umpire cannot re-call a live pitch, but on video the same umpire can call the same pitch on two days. The difference measures the umpire's own consistency, separate from the umpire's distance from the tracking system.

An LLM judge makes this test easy and more necessary. At temperature above zero the same prompt can produce a different verdict, and even at zero, batching and serving details can move outputs. Run every validation item through the judge several times and compute agreement or kappa between runs: that is its self-consistency. If the judge changes its verdict on 10% of items between identical calls, no comparison with humans can beat that noise. A cheap fix is the panel above: sample the judge three times, take the vote, and send the split items to a person. Unit 06 adds a second kind of repeat, swapping the order of two options, which tests a bias rather than noise.

The bar: humans against humans

How high should agreement be before a judge is trusted? Not 100%: people do not reach that either. The workable bar, which Igor's recipe also uses to grade the grader, is the one Zheng et al. 2023 used for MT-Bench: compare how often the model agrees with human experts against how often the experts agree with each other, on the same items with the same statistic. On first-turn questions with ties counted (chance agreement 33%), GPT-4 agreed with the experts 66% of the time and the experts with each other 63%. With ties dropped (chance 50%) the figures were 85% and 81%. On second-turn questions the experts edged GPT-4, 67% to 66%. These are raw percent agreements, the statistic this page warns about, and they rest on different numbers of votes. The fair reading: GPT-4 sat inside the human range on that task, not above it.

That is the main idea of the page. A judge that agrees with people about as well as people agree with each other is as good as the labels it is checked against; any further gain cannot be measured with those labels. A judge well below the human-human number is the weakest rater in the room. And a low human-human number says the policy is the problem, not the judge, which sends the work back to unit 02.

What two interviewers manage

Hiring researchers have measured how well interviewers agree, but this page cites no specific figure for it. The practical bar is local: compute Ana and Ben's kappa on your own loop before asking a judge to match them.

What two managers manage

A widely cited meta-analysis put the average interrater reliability of supervisors' overall job-performance ratings at 0.52, a correlation rather than a kappa, from 40 studies (Viswesvaran, Ones and Schmidt 1996). Later researchers dispute whether 0.52 is the right figure, a debate summarised in a 2019 meta-analysis, but nobody claims managers agree closely.

What two competitions manage

Across 13 U.S. competitions, 47% of the 2,440 wines entered in more than three of them won Gold somewhere, and 84% of those same wines won nothing at another; a Gold at one competition looked statistically independent of a Gold at the next (Hodgson 2009).

What two radiologists manage

Ten radiologists each read the same 150 mammograms. Between pairs, the median weighted percent agreement was 78%, a weighted kappa of 0.47, and the share of films from women without cancer that a reader sent for workup ranged from 11% to 65% (Elmore et al. 1994).

What two umpires manage

Taken pitches are called by one umpire, so this page cites no umpire-against-umpire figure. Umpiring has something better: the call can be checked against the tracking system, which is a validity question for unit 05.

The TriviaQA row is the warning in small. On an easy task where humans nearly always agree, percent agreement made most judges look fine; the chance-corrected statistic told them apart. Easy tasks raise the bar; on hard, subjective tasks the human-human number is the only honest ceiling.

Carry this away. Agreement is a reliability check, and it needs a chance correction to mean anything: report the grid, percent agreement, kappa (or alpha) and an interval, all on the same items. Watch the base rate: a rare YES makes percent agreement look great and kappa look awful at once, and the rare YES is usually what the judge is for. Then hold the judge to the human bar: about as consistent with people as people are with each other, measured the same way, and as consistent with itself as a repeat run allows.

What you can say now

  • Percent agreement counts chance matches as skill; on the casebook grid 75% agreement is only kappa 0.49, because 51% agreement was available by chance.
  • Kappa is the share of the room above chance that raters actually used, and chance comes only from each rater's own YES rate.
  • When YES is rare, 92% agreement can be kappa 0.29: the raters agree on the easy NO pile and split on the few cases that matter.
  • Fleiss' kappa handles a fixed panel of three or more; Krippendorff's alpha handles any number of raters, missing labels and ordered scales, and its common bar is 0.8, with 0.667 for tentative use.
  • On 100 items, kappa 0.49 could be anything from 0.32 to 0.66, so the Landis and Koch words cannot be told apart at that size.
  • Test a judge against itself first; then call it good enough when it agrees with people about as well as people agree with each other, as GPT-4 roughly did on MT-Bench (66% against 63% first turn, ties counted).
