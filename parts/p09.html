When not to trust a judge

A validated judge is validated for something: a policy, a population of items, a use. Step outside any of the three and the validation no longer covers you. Every older field keeps a list of the cases that go to someone else.
TL;DR — Seven situations break a judge. Humans do not agree on the policy, so there is nothing to validate against. The judge has a stake in what it grades: its own model family, or a system tuned against it. The items are unlike the gold set. The failure you care about is rare, so most flags are false. One item has to be decided, not five hundred averaged. The judged text contains instructions aimed at the judge. The judge's reasons contradict its score. In the first three, don't use the grade until you fix the cause. In the last four, use it as a flag that sends the item to a second look, never as the verdict. The older fields' remedies are the same everywhere: a second reader, a panel, an appeal, a committee.

Units 04 to 08 built a judge you can measure. This unit is about where that measurement stops. A judge that agrees with your labelers at kappa 0.6 on last month's gold set has earned that number on that set, for that policy, as an average. None of that transfers by default to a new product surface, to a model trained against the judge, or to the one case someone will be fired, rejected or sued over.

None of this is new either. Every institution that judges at scale has written down when the first judge's call is final and when it is not. A hiring process sends borderline or split candidates to a debrief or a hiring committee rather than letting one interviewer decide.A review process sends every rating through a calibration meeting, and the ratings that move a career get the most scrutiny.A wine competition seats judges in panels rather than alone, so no single palate decides a medal.European screening guidelines recommend reading every mammogram twice, with consensus or a third reader settling disagreements.Baseball lets a manager challenge a call on video, and since 2026 lets players challenge balls and strikes against the tracking system. Each rule is a decision about when not to trust the judge in front of you.

The decision in one picture

The order matters. The first three questions are about whether your validation applies at all. If humans cannot agree, or the judge is compromised, or the items are new, the kappa and sensitivity you measured in unit 08 describe some other situation. The last two questions assume the validation holds and ask whether the use you have in mind can live with the judge's known error rate. A judge that is 83.5% accurate is a fine instrument for some jobs and a dangerous one for others.

When there is nothing to check against

(a) The humans do not agree

A judge is validated against people. If two trained labelers applying your policy agree at kappa 0.49, as the two raters in unit 04 do, that is a ceiling. The model cannot be shown to be better than the humans, because the humans are the only yardstick. It can at most match them. When the headline of a judge paper is that the model agrees with people as often as people agree with each other, that is parity, and parity at a low number is still a low number. Zheng et al. 2023 report GPT-4 reaching over 80% agreement with human preferences, “the same level of agreement between humans”: a good result precisely because the human level was high enough to mean something.

When agreement is low the problem is the policy, not the judge. Two people reading the same output and disagreeing a quarter of the time are telling you the rubric leaves room for taste. Igor's recipe puts this step before any automation: make sure humans can apply the policy consistently. Automating a policy people cannot apply gives you a fast, cheap, confident version of the disagreement. Watch for the kappa paradox too: when failures are rare, two raters can agree on 92% of items and still sit at kappa 0.29. High raw agreement on a rare label is not evidence that the policy works.

Two interviewers, one candidate

Ana writes “hire”, Ben writes “no hire” on the same loop, and they split this way on a quarter of candidates. A model trained to imitate either of them inherits the split. Before automating, the fix is the scorecard: anchored answers for each question, so both of them score the same thing.

Two managers, one employee

Carla and Dev rate the same body of work “exceeds” and “meets”. In reviews there is no outside truth to settle it, so their disagreement is the whole story. A judge cannot be validated here until the rating definitions are sharp enough that informed managers converge.

Nothing beneath the panel

Wine quality has no outside truth, and the judges do not even agree with themselves reliably. In Hodgson's replicate-pour study at a major U.S. competition, only about 10 percent of judges kept the same wine within a single medal group across three blind pours, and another 10 percent sometimes scored the same wine anywhere from Bronze to Gold (Hodgson 2008). A model that matches these judges matches noise.

Ten readers, the same films

When ten radiologists each read the same 150 mammograms, their recommendations for immediate work-up on the films from women without cancer ranged from 11 to 65 percent, and pairs of readers agreed at a weighted kappa of 0.47 (Elmore et al. 1994). Medicine is lucky: it has biopsy and follow-up beneath the readers, so a judge can be checked against the outcome rather than against a reader.

The one domain that escapes

Umpires Ivo and June disagree on borderline pitches too, but here it does not matter for validation: the tracking system measures where the ball crossed the plate and the rulebook defines the zone. Human disagreement caps validation only where people are the truth. Behind the plate, the camera is.

(c) The items are off the map

A judge's numbers hold for items like the ones it was validated on. Change the task, the language, the product surface, the user population or the model producing the outputs, and the numbers are a hope. This is the ordinary failure of every test: a mammography reader's sensitivity is measured on a screening population and does not carry to a symptomatic clinic with a different base rate and different-looking cases. Two things make it worse for LLM judges. Outputs shift every time the system under test changes, so the gold set ages fast. And people's criteria move once they see new outputs: Shankar et al. 2024 call this criteria drift, the finding that grading outputs helps people define what they want, so the policy itself is partly a product of the outputs it was written against.

The remedy is cheap: label a fresh sample of 50 to 100 items from the new distribution and re-run the unit 08 checks before trusting a number. Until then, the judge's grade on a new kind of rolea new kind of joban unfamiliar stylea new kind of imagea new league's zone is an untested instrument.

The scorecard for a different job

An interviewer calibrated on hundreds of backend engineers is asked to interview a designer. The scorecard still produces numbers. Nobody knows what they mean until those hires have a year of performance behind them.

A rating for a role nobody has held

A manager rates the first person in a newly created role. The level expectations were written for other jobs, and the calibration committee has no past cases to compare against. The rating is a first draft of the standard, not an application of it.

A judge outside their region

A judge with a reliable palate for one region's reds is seated on a flight of a style they rarely taste. Their scores may be consistent and still miss what typicity means for that style. Competitions handle this by seating judges by category.

Same reader, different clinic

A reader's sensitivity and specificity were measured on routine screening films. Moved to a symptomatic clinic or a new imaging technology, the case mix and the images change, and so do the numbers. The old figures should not be carried over without a fresh audit.

A zone that moved

An umpire's accuracy is measured against the rulebook zone in one league. A league that defines the zone differently, or a new tracking setup, changes what “correct” means. Accuracy has to be measured again against the new definition.

When the judge has a stake, or the text fights back

(b) The judge grades its own side

Two ways a judge stops being independent. The first is direct: a judge grading outputs from its own model family. Panickssery et al. 2024 found that models can recognise their own generations at better than chance, and that the stronger that self-recognition, the stronger the self-preference: the judge scores its own text higher than human raters do. Any comparison of “our model versus theirs” graded by our model's sibling has this bias built in, and unit 07 covers it in detail.

The second is indirect and more common: the system was optimised against the judge. The moment you use a judge as a reward signal, a best-of-n selector or a hill-climbing target, the system learns whatever the judge rewards, including the parts of the judge that are wrong. Gao, Schulman and Hilton 2022 measured this for reward models: optimise hard enough against an imperfect proxy and the true quality it was standing in for stops improving, then falls.

When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.— Marilyn Strathern's phrasing of Goodhart's law, Strathern 1997

The practical rule: a judge's validation is stale the moment you train or tune against it. The gold set was labelled on outputs that had not yet learned the judge's blind spots. The new outputs have. Re-validate on fresh outputs from the optimised system, and keep a held-out judge, or a held-out set of human labels, that the optimisation never sees.

Candidates who learned the questions

Once a company's interview question bank leaks, candidates practise the answers. The scorecard still says “hire”; it just no longer measures what it measured when the questions were fresh. And an interviewer grading a candidate they personally referred is the self-preference case.

Work shaped to the rating sheet

When the rating definitions reward visible launches, employees learn to produce visible launches. The ratings rise; whether the work got better is a separate question the ratings can no longer answer. A manager rating the project they themselves championed has a stake in the grade.

A wine made for the medal

A producer who learns what panels reward can make a wine for the competition rather than for the table. The scores go up; the link between a medal and what drinkers value gets weaker. That is Goodhart in a glass.

Reading your own prior report

A radiologist reviewing a case they read last year anchors on their own earlier call. That is why audits and second reads use an independent reader who has not seen the first report.

Framing the pitch

Catchers learn to receive borderline pitches so they look like strikes to the umpire. The pitch has not moved; the judge's perception has been optimised against. Measured truth catches this, because the camera does not see the glove move.

(f) The judged text talks to the judge

A human reader can tell the difference between a document and instructions for reading it. A language model, by default, cannot reliably do so. If the text under evaluation is untrusted, written by a user, an outside model, a scraped page or an adversary, it can contain instructions aimed at the judge, and the judge may follow them. This has been demonstrated more than once. Raina, Liusie and Gales 2024 found short universal phrases that, appended to any text, inflate a judge's absolute scores, and that transfer to judges the attacker never saw; comparative judging was less exposed than absolute scoring. Shi et al. 2024 optimised an injected sequence that makes a pairwise judge pick the attacker's response, and found standard detection defences insufficient. Zhao et al. 2025 found that even trivial inputs, a lone punctuation mark or an opener like “Thought process:”, could earn false positive rewards from reference-based judges.

So if the content can be written by someone who benefits from a good grade, the grade is a flag, not a verdict. Delimit the judged text clearly, tell the judge it is data, compare against a reference where you can, and route anything that scores suspiciously well to a human. In the older fields this is the candidate who stuffs every answer with the scorecard's keywordsself-assessment written in the exact words of the next level's definitionwine doctored to mask a fault before it reaches the panelimage artefact that mimics or hides a lesioncatcher pulling the glove back into the zone: the thing being judged is working on the judge.

When one call matters

(d) The failure is rare and costly

Take the casebook judge from unit 05: sensitivity 0.80, specificity 0.85. On the casebook set, where 30% of items are truly YES, its YES calls are right 69.6% of the time. Now use the same judge to hunt a failure that occurs in 5% of items: a safety violation, a fabricated citation, a policy breach. Out of 1,000 items it catches 40 of the 50 real cases and flags 142.5 clean ones. Precision falls to 0.219. Nearly four of every five flags are false. Nothing about the judge changed; the base rate did.

Medicine solved the conceptual problem long ago with screening logic. A screening test is not a diagnosis. Its job is to decide who gets the expensive, accurate second test, and its numbers are set for that job: high sensitivity, accepting many false alarms, because the second test will clear them. A judge hunting rare, costly failures should be used the same way. Its flag is a reason for a second look, by a human or a stronger check, and never a verdict on its own. Two consequences follow. Report precision at the real base rate, not on a balanced gold set. And budget the reviewers before turning the judge on: at 5% prevalence, every 1,000 items produce about 180 flags for someone to read.

The rare dealbreaker

A reference check that flags possible dishonesty is a screen. Most flags have innocent explanations; a company that rejects on the flag alone loses good people. The flag triggers a conversation, not a decision.

The misconduct flag

A review comment hinting at a conduct problem is rare and serious. It goes to a separate process with its own evidence standard, not into the rating. The manager's impression is where an investigation starts, not where it ends.

Is it corked?

Cork taint is a rare fault, and a judge who suspects it will usually ask for a second bottle rather than marking the wine down. This is the one wine question with a lab-measurable truth, and competitions treat a taster's suspicion as a reason to check.

Screening, by design

A recall is not a cancer diagnosis. It sends the patient to further imaging and, if needed, biopsy. The screening read is tuned to catch, and the next steps are tuned to clear. Every flag is a second look by construction.

The game-deciding call

A call that ends a game is rare and costly. Baseball does not ask the plate umpire to be more careful on that pitch; it gives the teams a challenge so the call can be checked against tracking.

(e) One case versus five hundred

The same judge can be fit for one use and unfit for another, because errors behave differently in an average than in a single decision. On any one item the casebook judge is wrong 16.5% of the time, and its YES calls are wrong about 30% of the time. That is not good enough to decide anything important about one item. Averaged over many items, though, its errors become a predictable distortion. If the judge's error rates are the same for two systems, its measured pass rate is a straight-line function of the true one: 0.15 + 0.65 × true rate. Differences shrink to 65% of their true size, but they keep their sign and their order, and the true rate can be recovered from the measured one with the Rogan–Gladen correction used for screening tests.

This is why the usual LLM-judge use, comparing two prompts or two models over a few hundred items, is the use judges are best at, and why using the same judge to block a single user's message, grade a single student or reject a single contractor's work is a different and much harder claim. For the aggregate, report the noise: a confidence interval on the difference, as Igor's recipe asks in turn it into a benchmark and report the noise. With 500 items per system, a 10-point true gap shows up as 6.5 measured points, about two standard errors: real, but only just. Smaller gaps need more items. For the single case, use the judge as triage.

Why loops have several interviewers

No serious hiring process lets one interview decide. Several interviewers each see the candidate, and the decision is made over their combined notes. The single interviewer's score is an input to a decision, the way a judge's score should be.

The distribution and the person

A calibration committee can see that one team's ratings run high across forty people, a claim about an aggregate it can defend. Deciding that one specific person on that team is “meets” and not “exceeds” takes a discussion of that person's work.

A gold medal is a single item

Hodgson looked at over 4,000 wines across 13 U.S. competitions. Of the 2,440 wines entered in more than three, 47 percent won a Gold somewhere, and 84 percent of those same wines also won nothing at some other competition (Hodgson 2009). One medal is one judgement; the averages tell you more than any one award.

Programme audits versus patients

Screening programmes are audited on recall rates and cancer detection rates over thousands of reads, which is where a reader's numbers are reliable. For an individual patient, the read is the start of a pathway that ends in biopsy or follow-up.

Season accuracy, single pitch

An umpire who is right on the large majority of called pitches is an excellent umpire on a season's evidence. That says nothing about whether the 3–2 pitch in the ninth was right, which is why that one pitch can be challenged.

When the reasons and the score disagree

Most judge prompts ask for a rationale before the score. That is useful, since it lets a person audit the call, but it creates a check you should run: does the score follow from the reasons? A rationale listing two serious defects followed by 5/5, or praise followed by “fail”, is a judge contradicting itself. One of the two outputs is wrong, and you cannot tell which from the item alone. Treat any such item as a flag.

Do not treat agreement as proof either. Turpin et al. 2023 showed that chain-of-thought explanations can systematically misrepresent why a model reached its answer: when they biased models toward an answer, the models gave plausible reasoning for it without mentioning the bias. A judge's stated reason is evidence about its verdict, not a transcript of how it got there. Two cheap checks: sample items where reasons and score disagree and read them, and have a second model or a simple rule score the rationale alone, then compare.

The older fields know this one well. An interviewer whose notes describe a strong candidate but who writes “no hire” gets asked about it in the debrief, because something unwritten drove the call.A written review that describes “exceeds” work under a “meets” rating is the first thing a calibration committee asks about.Tasting notes full of praise next to a low score make a panel chair ask the judge to taste again.A report that describes a suspicious finding but assigns a benign BI-RADS category is a discrepancy that quality review is built to catch.An umpire's explanation of a ruling that cites the wrong rule is grounds for the crew chief to review it, whatever the call was.

What the older fields do instead

When a judge cannot be trusted alone, every field reaches for one of a handful of remedies. They all work the same way: add a second, independent look, and decide in advance how disagreements are settled.

The debrief and the committee

Many hiring processes collect independent written feedback before the debrief, so interviewers do not anchor on each other, and send split decisions to people who were not in the room. That is double reading plus arbitration, applied to people.

The calibration meeting

Managers bring proposed ratings to a room of peers who compare cases across teams, and ratings move when one manager's standard is out of line. With no outside truth to appeal to, the committee is the arbitration step, and it is what turns individual opinions into a shared policy.

Panels, not palates

Competitions seat judges in panels; in Hodgson's study each panel had four expert judges (Hodgson 2008). Pooling helps only so far: his analysis of variance found only about half of the panels gave awards based on wine quality alone. A jury averages away independent noise, not a shared blind spot.

Double reading, with arbitration

The European Commission Initiative on Breast Cancer suggests double reading, with consensus or arbitration for discordant reads, over single reading in screening programmes (a conditional recommendation, moderate certainty of evidence; ECIBC). A meta-analysis found double reading with arbitration raised cancer detection and lowered recall rates (Taylor and Potts 2008).

The challenge system

MLB has allowed managers to challenge many calls on replay since 2014, and from the 2026 season players can challenge balls and strikes against the Automated Ball-Strike system, keeping a challenge when it succeeds. In the 2026 regular season about 54% of the 10,557 challenges overturned the call (Automated Ball-Strike System). The human still calls every pitch; the machine is the appeal.

The LLM literature found the jury idea independently. Verga et al. 2024, “Replacing Judges with Juries”, replaced a single large judge with a panel of smaller models from different families. Across three judging settings and six datasets the panel did better than the single large judge, showed less bias toward any one model family, and cost over seven times less. The design choice that matters is the same one the wine data warns about: the panel members came from disjoint model families. Three copies of one model are one judge asked three times, so their errors are correlated and a vote does little. Diversity is what makes a jury a jury.

Two further rules carry over from every field. Decide the disagreement rule in advance, before seeing which way it would cut. And route the arbitrated cases back into the gold set: the items where judges split are exactly the items where your policy is least clear, and the ones most worth a human label. Igor's own red-pen review loop is this habit at its smallest: a human reading real output before any automation is trusted.

The trust checklist

One screen to run before relying on a judge's grade. Any “no” in the first block means don't use the number yet. Any “no” in the second means use it only as a flag.

Carry this away. A judge's validation covers one policy, one kind of item and one kind of use. Without human agreement, with a judge that has a stake, or on new items, the grade means nothing yet: fix the cause first. With rare failures, single decisions, hostile text or reasons that contradict the score, the grade is a flag that buys a second look. The older fields never let one judge decide alone when it mattered. They added an independent second reader, a rule for disagreements, and an appeal.

What you can say now

  • A judge cannot be shown to beat the humans it is validated against; low human kappa means fix the policy, not the judge.
  • Self-preference and optimisation pressure both break a judge's independence; once you train or hill-climb against a judge, its validation is stale.
  • Validation holds for items like the gold set; a new task, language or surface needs a fresh labelled sample first.
  • The same judge with sensitivity 0.80 and specificity 0.85 has precision 0.696 at 30% prevalence and 0.219 at 5%; for rare failures, its flags are a screen, not a verdict.
  • A judge can be fit to compare two systems over 500 items, since its errors shrink the gap without flipping it, and unfit to decide one case.
  • Text under evaluation can attack the judge; untrusted content gets flag-only treatment.
  • Double reading with arbitration, diverse panels, appeals and calibration committees are the standard remedies; a jury of one model family is not a jury.
