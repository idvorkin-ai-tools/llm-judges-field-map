The policy: what good means

Before asking whether a judge is right, someone has to write down what right means. That written policy is the ceiling on every judge that applies it, human or model.
TL;DR — An LLM judge applies a policy: the judge prompt plus its rubric. If two careful people would read that policy two ways, the model cannot agree with both; it quietly picks one, and nobody knows which. A usable policy is a short list of criteria, each with a way of scoring it, definitions, and worked examples that pin down where the lines fall. Yes/no checks are easier to apply the same way twice than a 1–5 scale, and a scale works only if every point has an anchor. Reading real outputs is how you find the criteria, and when raters split, the fix is usually a rewrite of the policy.

Every judging institution here runs on a document: scorecards, rating definitions, score sheets, a reporting standard, a rulebook. Without one a judge only reports taste, and taste does not transfer between judges.

For an LLM judge the document is the prompt. Treating it as a configuration detail (“rate how helpful this is from 1 to 10”) and spending the effort on the model gets the order backwards: the model can match people only as well as the instruction lets it, and the instruction is only as clear as its writers were about what they wanted.

The policy in hiring

A structured interview asks every candidate the same questions and gives the interviewer a scorecard: which skill each question probes, and what weak, adequate and strong answers sound like. Campion, Palmer and Campion counted fifteen components of structure and noted that virtually every earlier review found structure improves reliability and validity (Campion et al. 1997). The scorecard is the interviewer's judge prompt.

The policy in performance reviews

A review cycle comes with rating definitions: what “meets” and “exceeds” mean, usually per level. When each is one sentence (“consistently goes above and beyond”), every manager fills the gap with their own idea of above and beyond.

The policy at the tasting table

A wine competition hands each judge a score sheet. The UC Davis 20-point scale, built by Maynard Amerine's staff in 1959 for the university's experimental wines, splits points across about ten attributes, mostly fault checks (cloudiness, a vinegar smell, wrong colour) plus a small slot for overall quality. Robert Parker's 100-point scale, loosely modelled on school grades, became the consumer standard later (Davis guide, Wine rating).

The policy in the reading room

Breast imaging has BI-RADS, the American College of Radiology's reporting standard. Every mammogram report ends in a category from 0 to 6: 0 incomplete (call back for more views), 1 negative, 2 benign, 3 probably benign, 4 suspicious, 5 highly suggestive of malignancy, 6 known cancer. Each implies an action, so a report means the same thing at the next hospital (BI-RADS).

The policy behind the plate

The rulebook defines the strike zone: over home plate, from the hollow beneath the kneecap to the midpoint between the shoulders and the top of the uniform pants, judged from the batter's stance; any part of the ball through any part of the zone is a strike (Strike zone). Few policies are this exact, and umpires still do not call exactly this zone.

What a policy contains

Igor's evals recipe starts here, with domain experts writing quality guidelines before any automation. A policy has a short list of criteria, how each is scored (yes/no or 1–5), and enough detail and worked examples that a stranger would score the same way. Two more follow: definitions for any term a reader could take two ways, and a verdict rule that combines the criteria into pass or fail.

Criteria are mostly about what to leave out: a long list gets applied as the few each rater cares about. Definitions stop two readers meaning different things by one word; “faithful” can mean “nothing the source contradicts” or “nothing the source does not support”, and those grade many outputs differently. Anchors turn a description into something a stranger can apply: a real yes, a real no, and a borderline with its ruling. The verdict rule removes the last private choice, how much each criterion weighs.

For a support assistant answering from a product's documentation:

Verdict: passes the policy if all three are yes. Otherwise fails, and the failing criteria are reported, not just the verdict.

The last column matters most. Each ruling was a disagreement before it was written down, and will reappear in the judge's errors if left out; hence “lots of detail and worked examples”, not more criteria.

Yes/no checks or a 1–5 scale

A 1–5 scale looks richer: it can say “good but not great”. The cost is four boundaries instead of one, each a place where two readers can land on different sides, and each to be placed where every other rater places it.

Arbitrary Scoring Systems: Using uncalibrated scales (like 1-5) across multiple dimensions, where the difference between scores is unclear and subjective. What makes something a 3 versus a 4? Nobody knows, and different evaluators often interpret these scales differently.— Hamel Husain, Using LLM-as-a-Judge for Evaluation: A Complete Guide

Husain's advice is a domain expert's single pass/fail call per output, with a written critique. The critique keeps the nuance (“passed, but the second paragraph is padding”) without anyone agreeing whether that is a 3 or a 4. It is practice, not a measured result, but fewer boundaries mean fewer places to split. (Fewer categories also raise the agreement chance alone produces, which is why unit 04 corrects for chance.)

Stronger still: break one holistic grade into a yes/no check per criterion and let the verdict rule combine them. “Good answer, 1–5?” makes the rater weigh correctness against tone privately; “Answers the ask? Every claim supported? Next step safe?” makes the weighting public. A split on one check points at one definition.

Checks in the interview room

A good scorecard asks, per question, whether the candidate showed the skill it probes, and only then combines. A “strong hire” with no per-skill evidence is a number the committee cannot check or argue with.

Checks in the review cycle

Review forms carry a five-point rating, but calibration meetings argue one boundary at a time: is this employee over the line for exceeds? The yes/no question was the one that mattered.

Checks on the score sheet

The Davis sheet is mostly near-binary fault checks: appearance 2 if brilliant, 1 if clear but dull, 0 if cloudy; volatile acidity 2 if no vinegar smell, 1 if slight, 0 if obvious (a club copy of the scoring guide). It buys consistency by turning “how good is it” into “is anything wrong with it”.

Checks in the reading room

BI-RADS has seven categories, but each is tied to a near-yes/no action: 0 means call back, 4 and 5 mean biopsy. A policy that ends in an action is easier to apply than one that ends in an adjective.

Checks behind the plate

The strike zone is already yes/no checks: over the plate, above the knee hollow, below the torso midpoint. Every edge check passes or it is a ball; nobody awards “3 out of 5 strikiness”.

Scales earn their place when the decision has more than two outcomes, or when you want to move the threshold later: in unit 05 the casebook judge emits 1–5 and the best cut is “4 or above”. Such a scale needs anchors at every point.

Anchors: making each point mean something

A bare scale (“1 = poor, 5 = excellent”) gives every rater the same numbers and a different ruler. Industrial psychologists built a fix in the 1960s: behaviourally anchored rating scales, or BARS (Smith and Kendall 1963). Job experts collect real examples of effective and ineffective behaviour and sort them into dimensions. In a check called retranslation, a second group sorts the unlabelled examples back; those that do not return to their dimension are dropped, as are those raters disagree on rating. The survivors become the anchors (the steps).

The filtering is the useful idea for an LLM judge: an anchor is good not because it reads well but because independent readers place it in the same spot, and that can be tested.

The evidence on BARS is more modest than the idea. Anchored scales still suffer unreliability, leniency and poor separation between dimensions, and one early review suggested most of the value lies in the work of finding the dimensions, not the printed anchors (Schwab, Heneman and DeCotiis 1975, as summarised in Behaviorally anchored rating scales). For LLM judges too, a rubric's benefit comes mostly from its writers having to decide what they meant; a polished rubric copied from elsewhere skips that step.

Anchored answers in hiring

A structured scorecard anchors each question with sample weak, adequate and strong answers to, say, “tell me about a time a project slipped”. Without them, Ana's “adequate” and Ben's are two bars, and the outcome depends on which room the candidate walked into.

Vague definitions and leniency

Leniency (rating everyone high) and central tendency (everyone in the middle) are classic rating errors (Performance appraisal). A vague “exceeds” invites both: a protective manager can call almost anyone over the line, and Carla and Dev rate to different bars without either breaking the text. Concrete examples, by level, take that room away.

Anchors at the tasting table

The Davis colour criterion shows anchors must fit the thing judged: a Pinot Noir may verge on transparent where a Cabernet should be deep red. “Deep colour = good” would mark down every correct Pinot; Elodie and Femi need the anchor for the class in front of them.

Anchors in BI-RADS

BI-RADS category 4 is often split into 4A, 4B and 4C, anchored to rough cancer likelihoods (about 2–10%, 10–50% and 50–95%), because one “suspicious” label covered too wide a range (BI-RADS). An anchor stated as an outcome rate is about as sharp as anchors get.

Anchors behind the plate

The strike zone is anchored to the batter's body (knee hollow, torso midpoint), so one rule fits tall and short batters. Ivo and June still have to see those landmarks at speed, which is where their calls part from the rule.

One rubric per task, or one general judge

The cheapest judge prompt, “Rate how good this response is”, fits no task. A support reply (faithful, safe), a story (voice, pacing) and a SQL query (right rows) need different criteria. A general judge fills the gap with the model's idea of good: fluent, long, confident; unit 06 shows how that becomes length bias.

Use a shared frame with per-task content: the same structure everywhere (criteria, scoring, definitions, anchors, verdict rule, critique before verdict), with criteria and anchors written from each task's real outputs. A policy reused on another task is a new, unvalidated policy; its anchors were chosen for different borderlines.

Per role, not per company

One interview process, many scorecards: the frame is shared, the skills and anchored answers are written per role. A backend engineer's strong system-design answer is the wrong anchor for a designer.

Per level, not per company

Rating definitions are written per level: “exceeds” in a first job and for a senior lead are different scope. One company-wide definition becomes each manager's private version.

Per class, not per competition

The sheet is shared, but anchors move with the wine: correct colour, acidity and sweetness depend on the type, so wines are judged within their class. Dry-wine anchors would fail every dessert wine.

One frame, three modalities

BI-RADS, written for mammography, was adapted for ultrasound and MRI with nearly identical categories, so a 4 means the same next step on any machine: one frame, adapted per task.

One rule, every batter

The strike zone is one rule for every batter because it is defined relative to each stance: a per-item adjustment built in. Most LLM tasks are not that uniform, so they need their own anchors.

You find the criteria by grading

“Write the policy, then grade” does not survive real outputs. Shankar and colleagues watched people build LLM evaluators and named the problem:

users need criteria to grade outputs, but grading outputs helps users define criteria.— Shankar et al. 2024, Who Validates the Validators?

They called it criteria drift, and found some criteria depend on the outputs people happened to see. So the first draft is a guess. Read fifty real outputs and the third does something no rule covers: a correct answer citing a page that does not exist, a polite refusal that should have helped. Each surprise becomes a definition or an anchor. The policy is done when new outputs stop producing new rules.

So Igor's recipe puts human review before automation, and his blog review loop works the same way: he reads the rendered draft, marks the exact lines that are wrong, and sends the notes back, round after round. The post describes it as

step one of my evals recipe, a human reading real output before any automation— Igor Dvorkin, My CHOP setup: The Red Pen

Those notes are raw policy: a recurring note (a weak opener, post after post) is a criterion waiting to be written, and the lines it marked are its anchors.

Drift in the interview room

After a dozen interviews for a new role, the hard part turns out to be unlisted: candidates who solve the problem but cannot say why it works. “Explains the reasoning” becomes a criterion; the real candidates wrote that line.

Drift in the review cycle

The first calibration under new definitions spends an hour on one employee who hit every goal by leaving teammates stuck. The definitions said nothing about how goals were met; the next version does, with this case as the anchor.

Drift at the tasting table

The sheet says “balance”. Elodie marks an off-dry white down for sweetness; Femi calls it balanced for its style. Neither misapplied the sheet, because it never said. “Judge sweetness against the declared style” goes in before the next flight.

Drift in the reading room

BI-RADS is revised edition by edition: category 6 arrived in the fourth, and category 4 is now often subdivided. That is criteria drift at the scale of a profession, with the cases that did not fit writing the next edition.

Drift behind the plate

The strike zone has been redefined repeatedly since the 1880s; for 1969 it shrank to armpits-to-top-of-knees (Strike zone). Today's rule is the latest edition of a policy revised as the game changed.

Disagreement gets fixed in the policy

Step two of the recipe tests the policy: make sure humans can apply it consistently. Several people score the same cases; where they split, rewrite; repeat until agreement is high. Only then is there anything for an LLM judge to imitate.

Take the casebook's two raters: interviewers Ana and Benmanagers Carla and Devjudges Elodie and FemiDr Gale and Dr Hartumpires Ivo and June grade the same 100 items. They agree on 75 and split on 25: 15 where the first said hireexceedsmedalrecallstrike and the second did not, 10 the other way. Unit 04 shows that with chance agreement taken out, 75% is a kappa of about 0.49, only moderate (the arithmetic). This unit is about the 25.

Read them. Each split lands in one of three piles. A policy gap: the policy never addressed it, and each rater filled the gap differently. A definition split: it did, in a word they read differently. A rater slip: one rater missed something the policy clearly covers. Only the third is the rater's fault. The first two are fixed in the text: add the rule, sharpen the word, attach the item as an anchor. Then grade a fresh batch and count again.

Try thisSettle splits by rewriting the policy and watch kappa climb; the rater slips you leave unsettled are what remains.

The same holds once the model is in the loop. When the LLM judge disagrees with the humans, read those items before touching the model or prompt wording. Often the judge has found a real gap, something the humans settled by shared habit that the text never states; fixing the policy fixes it for both.

Splits in the debrief

Ana and Ben split on 25 of 100 candidates, mostly over one question: can a candidate who needed a hint score “strong”? The scorecard never said. “A hint caps the answer at adequate” settles a whole pile at once, for every interviewer after them.

Splits in calibration

Calibration is this loop by committee. Carla and Dev disagree on 25 of 100; many turn on whether a project cancelled by a reorganisation counts against its owner. The ruling goes into next cycle's definitions, or the same argument happens again.

Splits at the table

Elodie and Femi disagree on 25 of 100 wines. Several are the same off-dry style scored down by one and up by the other, fixed by a line on the sheet; a few are faults one missed, rater slips that replicate pours (unit 04) trace to a judge.

Splits in double reading

Dr Gale and Dr Hart differ on 25 of 100 recall decisions. Some are a missed finding, which double reading exists to catch. Others are one finding called a 3 by one and a 0 by the other: a policy question, which anchored categories let them argue with the text open.

Written zone, called zone

Ivo and June disagree with each other, but the bigger gap is between both and the rulebook. Many believe the called zone moved toward the rulebook after pitch tracking began grading umpires in the early 2000s (Strike zone). The written policy did not change; measuring the gap did.

An LLM judge has the same gap. The prompt says “do not reward length” and grades still rise with length; it says “supported by the source” and the judge passes true but unsupported answers. The prompt is the written zone; only a labelled set shows the called one.

Carry this away. The judge prompt is a policy, and the policy is the ceiling. Few criteria, yes/no unless a scale is needed, every ambiguous word defined, every point anchored with real examples and ruled borderlines. Expect to find criteria by reading outputs, and when raters split, rewrite the text before blaming the raters or the model.

What you can say now

  • A policy has five parts: criteria, scoring, definitions, anchors with ruled borderlines, and a verdict rule.
  • Yes/no has one boundary to agree on; 1–5 has four. Per-criterion checks make a disagreement point at one definition.
  • BARS (Smith and Kendall 1963) tie each scale point to real examples that independent experts place consistently; the clearer benefit is the work of writing them.
  • A shared frame with per-task criteria and anchors beats one general “rate this 1–10” judge.
  • Criteria drift (Shankar et al. 2024): grading is how you find the criteria, so humans read real outputs before anything is automated.
  • Read the splits; policy gaps and fuzzy definitions are fixed in the text. Written and applied policy always differ; only measurement shows how much.
