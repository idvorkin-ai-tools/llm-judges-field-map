The policy: what good means

Before anyone can ask whether a judge is right, someone has to write down what right means. The written policy is the ceiling on every judge that applies it, human or model.
TL;DR — An LLM judge applies a policy: the judge prompt plus its rubric. If two careful people reading that policy would grade the same output differently, the model cannot agree with both of them: it will quietly pick one reading, and nobody will know which, because the disagreement is in the text, not in the reader. A usable policy is a short list of criteria, each with a stated way of scoring it, definitions of the terms it uses, and worked examples that pin down where the lines fall. Yes/no checks per criterion are usually easier to apply the same way twice than a 1–5 scale, and a scale only works if every point on it has an anchor. You cannot write the policy in advance and be done: reading real outputs is how you find out what the criteria are. When raters split, the fix is usually a rewrite of the policy.

Every judging institution in this course runs on a document. Hiring panels have scorecards, review cycles have rating definitions, wine competitions have score sheets, breast radiologists have a reporting standard, and umpires have a rulebook. The document comes first because a judge without one is only reporting taste, and taste does not transfer from one judge to the next.

For an LLM judge the document is the prompt. It is tempting to treat that prompt as a configuration detail, a sentence like “rate how helpful this response is from 1 to 10”, and to spend the effort on the model. That gets the order backwards. The model can only match people as well as the instruction lets it, and the instruction can only be as clear as the people who wrote it were about what they wanted.

The policy in hiring

A structured interview gives every candidate for a role the same questions and gives the interviewer a scorecard: which skills each question probes, and what a weak, adequate and strong answer sounds like. A review of the research by Campion, Palmer and Campion counted fifteen separate components of structure, some about what is asked and some about how answers are evaluated, and noted that virtually every earlier review had found structure improves an interview's reliability and validity (Campion et al. 1997). The scorecard is the interviewer's judge prompt.

The policy in performance reviews

A review cycle comes with rating definitions: what “meets” and “exceeds” mean, usually written per level, so that a manager rating a junior engineer and a manager rating a senior one are measuring against the right bar. When those definitions are a sentence each (“consistently goes above and beyond”), every manager fills in the gap with their own idea of above and beyond.

The policy at the tasting table

A wine competition hands each judge a score sheet. The best-known older one is the UC Davis 20-point scale, developed by Maynard Amerine and his staff in 1959 to rate the large number of experimental wines the university was making. It splits the points across about ten attributes, most of them checks for faults (cloudiness, a vinegar smell, wrong colour for the type) plus a small slot for overall quality. The 100-point scale popularised by Robert Parker, with its 50–100 range loosely modelled on school grades, became the common consumer scale later (Davis guide, Wine rating).

The policy in the reading room

Breast imaging has BI-RADS, a reporting standard published by the American College of Radiology. Every mammogram report ends in one assessment category from 0 to 6: 0 incomplete (call the patient back for more views), 1 negative, 2 benign, 3 probably benign, 4 suspicious, 5 highly suggestive of malignancy, 6 known cancer. Each category implies an action, so a report from one hospital means the same thing at the next (BI-RADS).

The policy behind the plate

The rulebook defines the strike zone: over home plate, from the hollow beneath the batter's kneecap up to the midpoint between the top of the shoulders and the top of the uniform pants, judged from the batter's stance, and a pitch is a strike if any part of the ball passes through any part of that zone (Strike zone). Few judging policies are this exact, and umpires still do not call exactly this zone, as the last section shows.

What a policy contains

Igor's evals recipe starts here: before any automation, sit with people who know the domain and write the quality guidelines. The list it gives is short and worth taking literally. A policy has a short list of criteria, how each one is scored (yes/no or a 1–5 scale), and enough detail and worked examples that a stranger would score the same way. Add two things the list implies: definitions for any term a reader could take two ways, and a verdict rule that says how the per-criterion scores combine into the final pass or fail.

The parts do different jobs. The criteria say what is being looked at, which is mostly a matter of what to leave out: a long list of criteria tends to be applied as the few each rater happens to care about, and those differ from rater to rater. The scoring line says what kind of answer each criterion takes. The definitions stop two readers meaning different things by the same word; “faithful” can mean “says nothing the source contradicts” or “says nothing the source does not support”, and those grade a good share of outputs differently. The anchors are where the policy turns from a description into something a stranger can apply: a real example of a yes, a real no, and the borderline case with the ruling written next to it. The verdict rule removes the last private choice, which is how much each criterion weighs.

Here is what that looks like for a plain LLM task, a support assistant answering questions from a product's documentation:

The interesting column is the last. Each of those rulings was a disagreement before it was written down, and each one will reappear in the judge's errors if it is left out. That is why the recipe asks for “lots of detail and worked examples” and not a longer list of criteria.

Yes/no checks or a 1–5 scale

The scoring line is a real choice. A 1–5 scale looks richer: it can say “good but not great”. The cost is four boundaries instead of one, and every boundary is a place where two readers can land on different sides. On a single yes/no question a rater has one line to locate; on a five-point scale a rater has to locate four, and has to place them where every other rater places them too.

Arbitrary Scoring Systems: Using uncalibrated scales (like 1-5) across multiple dimensions, where the difference between scores is unclear and subjective. What makes something a 3 versus a 4? Nobody knows, and different evaluators often interpret these scales differently.— Hamel Husain, Using LLM-as-a-Judge for Evaluation: A Complete Guide

Husain's practitioner advice is to start with a domain expert making a single pass/fail call on each output, with a written critique saying why. The critique keeps the nuance a scale was supposed to capture (“passed, but the second paragraph is padding”) without asking anyone to agree on whether that is a 3 or a 4. This is advice from practice, not a measured result, but the logic is simple: fewer boundaries mean fewer places for two raters to split. (Fewer categories also raise the agreement chance alone would produce, which is why unit 04 corrects for chance.)

The stronger form of the idea is to break one holistic grade into several yes/no checks, one per criterion, and let the verdict rule combine them. “Is this a good answer, 1–5?” asks the rater to weigh correctness against tone against completeness privately. “Does it answer the ask? Is every claim supported? Is the next step safe?” asks three questions each with one line to find, and makes the weighting public. When raters disagree on the holistic grade, nobody can tell why; when they disagree on one check, the disagreement points at one definition.

Checks in the interview room

A good scorecard does not ask for “overall impression, 1–5” at the end of the hour. It asks, per question, whether the candidate showed the skill the question was written to probe, and only then combines. An interviewer who writes “strong hire” with no per-skill evidence has given the hiring committee a number it cannot check or argue with.

Checks in the review cycle

Review forms often carry a five-point overall rating, but calibration meetings tend to argue about one boundary at a time: is this employee over the line for exceeds or not? That is a yes/no question being asked of a scale, which is a sign the yes/no question was the one that mattered.

Checks on the score sheet

Read the Davis sheet closely and most of it is a set of nearly binary fault checks. In one circulated version, appearance gets 2 points if the wine is brilliant, 1 if clear but dull, 0 if cloudy; volatile acidity gets 2 if there is no vinegar smell, 1 if slight, 0 if obvious (a club copy of the scoring guide). Only the small “general quality” slot is left to open judgement. The sheet buys consistency by turning most of “how good is it” into “is anything wrong with it”.

Checks in the reading room

BI-RADS has seven categories, but each one is tied to an action, and the actions are close to yes/no: does this patient come back for more imaging, and does this finding get a biopsy? Category 0 means call back; 4 and 5 mean biopsy. A policy that ends in an action is easier to apply than one that ends in an adjective, because everyone knows what hangs on the line.

Checks behind the plate

The strike zone is already a set of yes/no checks: is the ball over the plate, is it above the hollow of the knee, is it below the midpoint of the torso? A pitch is a strike only if every edge check passes. Umpires do not award “3 out of 5 strikiness”, and nobody would want them to.

Scales still earn their place when the decision downstream really has more than two outcomes, or when a score will be thresholded later and you want the freedom to move the threshold. Unit 05 uses exactly that: the casebook judge emits a 1–5 score, and the best cut turns out to be “4 or above”. A scale used that way needs anchors at every point, which is the next section.

Anchors: making each point mean something

A bare scale (“1 = poor, 5 = excellent”) gives every rater the same numbers and a different ruler. Industrial psychologists met this in the 1960s with performance ratings and built a fix: behaviourally anchored rating scales, or BARS. Patricia Cain Smith and L. M. Kendall described the method in 1963 (Smith and Kendall 1963). People who know the job collect real examples of effective and ineffective behaviour and sort them into dimensions. Then comes the check they called retranslation: a second group of experts, given the examples without their labels, has to sort them back into the right dimension, and examples that do not come back where they started are dropped. The survivors are rated for how effective they are, examples the raters disagree on are dropped too, and what is left becomes the anchors at each point of the scale (the steps).

Those filtering steps are the useful idea for an LLM judge. An anchor is not good because it reads well; it is good because independent readers place it in the same spot. That can be tested.

The evidence on BARS themselves is more modest than the idea. Reviews found that anchored scales can still suffer from unreliability, leniency and poor separation between dimensions, and one early review suggested much of their value lies in the work of finding the dimensions rather than in the anchors as printed (Schwab, Heneman and DeCotiis 1975, as summarised in Behaviorally anchored rating scales). That is worth keeping in mind for LLM judges too. The benefit of a rubric comes mostly from the people who wrote it having had to decide what they meant. A beautifully formatted rubric copied from somewhere else skips that step.

Anchored answers in hiring

A structured interview scorecard anchors each question with sample answers: what a weak, adequate and strong response to “tell me about a time a project slipped” actually sounds like. Without them, Ana's “adequate” and Ben's “adequate” are two different bars, and the candidate's outcome depends on which room they walked into.

Vague definitions and leniency

“Leniency error”, rating everyone high, and “central tendency”, rating everyone in the middle, are two of the classic rating errors (Performance appraisal). A vague definition invites both: when “exceeds” has no concrete examples, a manager who wants to protect their team can call almost anyone over the line, and Carla and Dev end up rating to different bars without either being wrong by the text. Concrete examples of exceeds work, by level, take that room away.

Anchors at the tasting table

The Davis sheet's colour criterion shows why anchors have to be specific to the thing judged. The guide says acceptable colour depends heavily on the grape: a Pinot Noir may be light enough to verge on transparent, where a Cabernet should be deep red. An anchor of “deep colour = good” would mark down every correct Pinot. Elodie and Femi need the anchor for the class in front of them.

Anchors in BI-RADS

BI-RADS goes further than labels. Category 4 is often split into 4A, 4B and 4C, anchored to rough ranges of cancer likelihood (about 2–10%, 10–50% and 50–95%), because in some experts’ view a single “suspicious” category carries too wide a range of risk (BI-RADS). An anchor stated as an outcome rate is about as sharp as an anchor gets.

Anchors behind the plate

The strike zone is anchored to the batter's body, not to a fixed height: the knee hollow and the torso midpoint, judged from the batting stance. That makes the zone the same rule for a tall batter and a short one, which a fixed height could not. Ivo and June still have to see those landmarks at speed, which is where their calls part from the rule.

One rubric per task, or one general judge

The cheapest judge prompt is general: “Rate how good this response is.” It works for no task in particular. The criteria that matter for a support reply (faithful to the docs, safe next step) are not the criteria for a story (voice, pacing) or for a SQL query (returns the right rows). A general judge fills the gap with the model's own idea of good, which tends to mean fluent, long and confident; unit 06 shows how that turns into length and style bias.

The practical answer is a small shared frame with per-task content. Keep the structure the same everywhere: criteria, scoring line, definitions, anchors, verdict rule, and a written critique before the verdict. Write the criteria, definitions and anchors per task, from that task's real outputs. A policy written for one task and reused for another should be treated as a new, unvalidated policy, because its anchors were chosen for different borderline cases.

Per role, not per company

A company has one interview process but many scorecards. The frame (same questions for every candidate for a role, rate each answer against anchors, decide only after all the evidence is in) is shared; the skills and the anchored answers are written per role. A backend engineer's “strong answer” on system design is the wrong anchor for a designer.

Per level, not per company

Rating definitions are usually written per level for the same reason. “Exceeds” for someone in their first job and “exceeds” for a senior lead are different amounts of scope. One company-wide definition gets applied as each manager's private version of the levels.

Per class, not per competition

The score sheet is shared across a competition, but the anchors move with the wine. What counts as correct colour, acidity or sweetness depends on the type in the glass, so wines are judged against others of their kind. A dessert wine scored with dry-wine anchors would fail on sweetness every time.

One frame, three modalities

BI-RADS was written for mammography and later adapted for ultrasound and MRI. The summary of each assessment category is nearly identical across the three, so a 4 means the same next step whichever machine produced the image. One shared frame, adapted per task.

One rule, every batter

The strike zone is one rule applied to every batter, and it stays usable because it is defined relative to each batter's stance. The general rule carries a per-item adjustment built in. Most LLM tasks are not that uniform, which is why they need their own anchors.

You find the criteria by grading

The tidy version of all this, “write the policy, then grade”, does not survive contact with real outputs. Shankar and colleagues watched people build LLM evaluators with an assisted tool and named the problem:

users need criteria to grade outputs, but grading outputs helps users define criteria.— Shankar et al. 2024, Who Validates the Validators?

They called it criteria drift. They also found that some criteria seemed to depend on the particular outputs people had seen, rather than being definable in advance. In practice that means the first draft of a policy is a guess. You read fifty real outputs, and the third one does something nobody wrote a rule for: it answers correctly but cites a page that does not exist, or it refuses politely when it should have helped. Each surprise becomes a definition or an anchor. The policy is finished when new outputs stop producing new rules, not when the first draft reads well.

That is why Igor's recipe puts human review before any automation, and why his own blog review loop is built the same way. He reads the rendered draft, marks the exact lines that are wrong, and sends the notes back for revision, round after round. The post describes it as

step one of my evals recipe, a human reading real output before any automation— Igor Dvorkin, My CHOP setup: The Red Pen

The notes from a loop like that are raw material for a policy. Each recurring note (say, a weak opener flagged on post after post) is a criterion waiting to be written down, and the lines it was attached to are its anchors.

Drift in the interview room

A team writes a scorecard for a new role, runs a dozen interviews, and finds the hard part was something nobody listed: candidates who solve the problem but cannot say why their answer works. After the debriefs, “explains the reasoning” becomes a criterion with its own anchored answers. The real candidates wrote that line of the scorecard.

Drift in the review cycle

The first calibration meeting under new definitions spends an hour on one case: an employee who hit every goal by working alone and leaving teammates stuck. The definitions said nothing about how goals were met. The next version does, with this case, anonymised, as the anchor.

Drift at the tasting table

A panel's sheet says “balance”. The first flight includes a deliberately off-dry white, and Elodie marks it down for sweetness while Femi calls it balanced for its style. Nobody can say who applied the sheet wrongly, because the sheet never said. The ruling (judge sweetness against the declared style) is written in before the next flight.

Drift in the reading room

Reporting categories are revised edition by edition as experience accumulates: BI-RADS added category 6 in its fourth edition, and category 4 is now often subdivided because one “suspicious” label covered too wide a range of risk. That is criteria drift at the scale of a profession, with the cases that did not fit writing the next edition.

Drift behind the plate

The strike zone itself has been redefined more than once since the first rule defining one in the 1880s; for the 1969 season, for example, it was shrunk to run from the armpits to the top of the knees (Strike zone). The rule umpires carry today is the latest edition of a policy revised as the game changed.

Disagreement gets fixed in the policy

Step two of the recipe is where a policy is tested: make sure humans can apply it consistently. Several people score the same cases, you measure how often they agree, and where they split, you rewrite the policy. Repeat until agreement is high. Only then is there anything for an LLM judge to imitate.

Take the casebook's two raters: interviewers Ana and Benmanagers Carla and Devjudges Elodie and FemiDr Gale and Dr Hartumpires Ivo and June grade the same 100 items. They agree on 75 and split on 25: 15 where the first said hireexceedsmedalrecallstrike and the second did not, 10 the other way round. Unit 04 shows that once chance agreement is taken out, 75% is a kappa of about 0.49, which is only moderate (the arithmetic). This unit is about what to do with the 25.

Read them. Each split usually falls into one of three piles. A policy gap: the item raises something the policy never addressed, and each rater filled the gap differently. A definition split: the policy addressed it, but in a word the two raters read differently. A rater slip: one rater simply missed something the policy clearly covers. Only the third pile is the rater's fault. The first two are fixed by editing the text: add the missing rule, sharpen the word, attach the item as an anchor with its ruling. Then grade a fresh batch and count again.

The same logic applies after the model is in the loop. When the LLM judge disagrees with the humans, read those items before changing the model or the prompt wording. Often the judge has found a real gap in the policy, an item the humans settled by shared habit that the text never states. Fixing that in the policy fixes it for the humans and the model together.

Splits in the debrief

Ana and Ben interview the same candidates and split on 25 of 100. Reading the 25, most turn out to hinge on one question: whether a candidate who needed a hint can still score “strong”. The scorecard never said. Writing the rule down (a hint caps the answer at adequate) settles a whole pile of splits at once, for every interviewer after them.

Splits in calibration

A calibration meeting is this loop run by committee. Carla and Dev disagree on 25 of 100 employees; the committee reads the cases, finds that many turn on whether a reorganisation-cancelled project counts against its owner, and writes the ruling into next cycle's definitions. Without that last step, the same argument happens again next cycle.

Splits at the table

Elodie and Femi disagree on 25 of 100 wines. Several of the splits are the same off-dry style marked down by one and up by the other, and a few are faults one of them did not notice. The first kind is fixed by a line on the sheet; the second is a rater slip, and replicate pours (unit 04) show which judge it belonged to.

Splits in double reading

Dr Gale and Dr Hart read the same 100 screens and differ on 25 recall decisions. Some are a finding one of them missed, which is what double reading is for. Others are the same finding called a 3 by one and a 0 by the other. Those are a policy question, and a shared standard with anchored categories is what turns them from opinion into something the two can argue about with the text open.

Written zone, called zone

Ivo and June can disagree with each other, but the bigger gap is between both of them and the rulebook. The enforced zone varies with the umpire's view, and many umpires, players and analysts believe the called zone moved closer to the rulebook after pitch tracking began grading umpires in the early 2000s (Strike zone). The written policy did not change. Measuring the gap did.

An LLM judge has exactly this gap. The prompt says “do not reward length”, and the judge's grades still rise with length. The prompt says “faithful means supported by the source”, and the judge passes answers that are true but unsupported. Reading the prompt tells you the written zone; only grading a labelled set tells you the called one.

Carry this away. The judge prompt is a policy, and the policy is the ceiling. Write a short list of criteria, score each one yes or no unless a scale is truly needed, define every word two people could read two ways, and anchor every point with real examples including ruled borderlines. Expect to find many of the criteria by reading outputs. When raters split, read the splits and rewrite the text before blaming the raters, the model or the prompt wording.

What you can say now

  • A policy has five parts: a short list of criteria, how each is scored, definitions, anchors including ruled borderline cases, and a verdict rule that combines the criteria.
  • Yes/no per criterion has one boundary to agree on; a 1–5 scale has four. Splitting a holistic score into several checks makes disagreements point at a single definition.
  • Behaviourally anchored rating scales (Smith and Kendall 1963) tie each scale point to real examples, kept only if independent experts place them consistently. The evidence that the anchors themselves help is mixed; the work of writing them is the clearer benefit.
  • A shared frame with per-task criteria and anchors beats one general “rate this 1–10” judge, which falls back on the model's own taste.
  • Criteria drift (Shankar et al. 2024): grading is how you find the criteria, so humans read real outputs before anything is automated.
  • Where two raters split, read the splits; policy gaps and fuzzy definitions are fixed by editing the policy. The written policy and the applied one always differ; only measurement shows by how much.
