Calibrating and validating a judge

A checklist you could run tomorrow. Hiring panels, review committees, wine shows, breast screening programmes and umpire supervisors already run every step on their human judges.
TL;DR — Validate an LLM judge the way you would qualify a new human one. Write the policy and have people label real outputs first; how often they agree with each other is the bar. Their agreed labels become the gold set. Tune the judge on one slice of it, test once on another, and report YES and NO items separately. Swap positions, re-run items and pad answers to catch the known biases. Put an error bar on every number; at 100–200 items it is wide. Let the judge settle the clear cases, send the uncertain middle to people, and keep auditing, because judge, prompt and traffic all drift.

Units 04 to 07 gave the measurements and the failure modes; this unit puts them in order. The order matters most: broken judges usually were validated, just against labels nobody checked, on the items the prompt was tuned on.

1. Write the policy, then have people label real outputs

The first artefact is the policy, not a judge prompt: a short list of criteria, how each is scored, and enough worked examples that a stranger would score the same way. Igor's recipe makes this step one, with human review set up before any automation, since everything later is checked against it. Unit 02 covers writing the policy.

Then people label real outputs. Read 50 to 100, write in plain words why each failing one fails, and only then group the notes into criteria (Igor's AI testing). Hamel Husain starts the same way: one domain expert gives each item a pass/fail and a written critique. The critiques later become the judge's few-shot examples, so a new hire should be able to follow them.

Expect the policy to change while you label. Shankar and colleagues hit this building EvalGen, a tool that picks automatic evaluators by how well they match a person's grades, and called it criteria drift:

users need criteria to grade outputs, but grading outputs helps users define criteria.— Shankar et al. 2024, Who Validates the Validators?

So first-pass labels are drafts. Re-label the early items once the policy settles, or the gold set holds two policies at once.

Hiring: the scorecard before the interviews

A structured loop writes the scorecard first: the questions, plus anchored strong, middling and weak answers. New interviewers practise on recorded interviews against the anchors. Their “hire” counts only after that.

Reviews: definitions and exemplar packets

A review process defines “exceeds” and “meets” per level, then collects exemplar packets: anonymised half-years of work a committee placed at each rating. Managers read them before rating, as the judge prompt carries its few-shot critiques.

Wine: the scoring sheet and palate alignment

A wine show fixes its scoring sheet before the first glass. Australia's Advanced Wine Assessment Course, for would-be show judges, opens with fault training and then “palate alignment brackets”: score wines, compare with guest judges (AWAC course notes). Policy and first labelling pass, with wine.

Medicine: the reporting categories come first

Breast imaging fixed its vocabulary before auditing anyone: BI-RADS categories say what each verdict means and what happens next. A “recall” can be audited because everyone means the same thing by it.

Umpires: the zone is written down

The rulebook defines the strike zone before any pitch is called. That short, precise policy is why a machine can check every call against it.

2. Measure human agreement on a gold set: that is the bar

First ask how often two careful people agree. Two labellers grade the same items independently; compute agreement with chance removed (unit 04). Where they split, rewrite the policy and relabel until the number stops rising (Igor's step 2). Agreed items plus discussed splits become the gold set.

That number is the bar. In the casebook, interviewers Ana and Benmanagers Carla and Devjudges Elodie and FemiDr Gale and Dr Hartumpires Ivo and June agree on 75 of 100 items: Cohen's kappa 0.49. A judge that agrees with either of them that well has reached human level; asking for more asks it to know what neither person knows. Igor's rule: trust a judge that agrees with humans about as well as humans agree with each other, measured the same way on the same items.

The unit 05 judge agrees with the gold labels at kappa about 0.62, above the 0.49 between two single raters: a label two people agreed on is less noisy than either alone. So compare like with like: judge-versus-Ana beside Ben-versus-Ana; judge-versus-gold beside a third human against the same gold.

Hiring: shadowing and calibration

Shadowing: the new interviewer sits in, scores privately, compares with the experienced one afterwards, then they swap. Where they keep splitting, the anchors get rewritten. Ana and Ben's 75% is what a team would see if both scored the same candidates blind.

Reviews: the calibration meeting

The calibration meeting is this step in a room: managers defend proposed ratings and the group moves the outliers. Carla and Dev rating the same packets beforehand, without talking, gives the agreement number; the meeting's output is the gold label.

Wine: the panel and its splits

Panels score each glass independently, then compare. Elodie and Femi agreeing on 75 of 100 wines is the panel's reliability; the splits get discussed. With no outside truth about quality, the panel's agreed score is the gold label.

Medicine: double reading

Some programmes have two readers look at every mammogram independently: standard in the United Kingdom, not in the United States, where insurers do not pay for it (Mammography). It yields a running human–human agreement number for free.

Umpires: two umpires, the same video

Live, one umpire calls each pitch; a human–human bar would need Ivo and June on the same video. Baseball rarely needs it, because tracking measures the truth directly: the one domain that can skip to step 3 against a machine.

3. Treat the judge as a classifier: split, tune, test once

A pass/fail judge is a binary classifier and the gold set is its labelled data. Hamel Husain's split (also in Igor's AI testing):

  • Train. 10–20% of the labels: clear passes and fails with critiques. The only items allowed in the prompt.
  • Dev. Run the judge, read every disagreement with the humans, fix the prompt, repeat as often as you like.
  • Test. A held-out set, run once when you think you are done; that is the number you report. Tune against it and it measures nothing. Hamel puts 40–45% each in dev and test, with 30 to 50 items of each label in each.

Three rules go with the split.

Report sensitivity and specificity separately, next to the human number. One accuracy figure hides which way the judge errs; with a 5% failure rate, Hamel notes, always-pass scores 95% and catches nothing. The unit 05 judge shows the right report: 48 of 60 YES items caught (0.80), 119 of 140 NO items cleared (0.85).

Critique before verdict. The judge writes its reasoning first and its label last, like the human critiques it learned from. Evidence before rating is one of Wang et al.'s fixes for a judge that is easy to sway (Wang et al. 2023), and the critique is what you read in dev when judge and humans split.

Pin the model version. A judge is the prompt and the model. Name the exact snapshot in config so this month's scores mean what last month's did; a new model is a new judge, back to the test set. Hamel's answer to “How do you ensure consistency when updating your LLM model?” is the same: run the process again.

Hiring: who checks the new interviewer

A new interviewer is checked against experienced ones on shared candidates, and their scorecards are read: a “no hire” with no written reason is sent back. That reason is the critique; reading it catches rejections for the wrong thing.

Reviews: the first-time manager

A first-time manager's ratings are compared with what the committee settles on. If Carla's “exceeds” keeps moving down, what matters is the direction she errs: sensitivity and specificity, review-style.

Wine: scoring the judge, not just the wine

The AWAC scores each participant's own tasting for discrimination (good wines from weak), reliability (same score on a second tasting) and stability (drifting up or down the second time) (AWAC course notes). A judge evaluated as an instrument.

Medicine: sensitivity and specificity were born here

Diagnostic medicine describes a test or reader with sensitivity and specificity, not one accuracy, because missing a cancer and recalling a healthy patient cost such different things.

Umpires: graded call by call

MLB grades plate umpires against pitch tracking, with a small buffer around the zone, reported to have shrunk for 2025 from two inches to three-quarters of an inch (CBS Sports, relaying The Athletic). Wrong strikes and wrong balls are different errors, so a serious report keeps them apart.

4. Run the bias checks

A judge that passes the test set can still be pushed around by things unrelated to quality (units 06 and 07). Three checks are cheap enough for every new judge.

Swap positions. Show every pair in both orders and count only verdicts that survive; this catches position bias. With ChatGPT as evaluator, just changing the order let Vicuna-13B beat ChatGPT on 66 of 80 queries (Wang et al. 2023). In the casebook swap from unit 06, 62 of 100 pairs keep their verdict and the other 38 lean toward whichever came first (30 to 8): those 38 are ties.

Re-run the same items. Grade the test set again with production settings and count changed verdicts. This self-consistency caps everything else: a judge that disagrees with itself on an item cannot be reliably right about it. If the flip rate is high, take a majority of several runs or lower the temperature, and say which.

Length-controlled pairs. Pair an answer with itself plus padding: restated content, near-duplicate points, a longer preamble. A judge that prefers the padded one shows length bias. Do the same for confident tone or markdown.

Hiring: order and polish

Position bias is interview order; padding is the candidate who talks longest and most fluently. Structured scorecards exist partly so polish does not win on polish. Re-scoring a recorded interview a week later is the re-run most teams never do.

Reviews: recency and volume

A half-year read in one sitting gets recency bias: the last month weighs most. Padding is the self-assessment that lists everything. Re-rating an anonymised packet from last cycle is the re-run.

Wine: replicate pours

Wine invented the re-run. From 2005 to 2008 Hodgson had the California State Fair hide triplicate pours of one bottle in judges' flights of 30. Only about 10% of judges kept the same wine within one medal group each time, and another 10% sometimes scored it Bronze and Gold (Hodgson 2008). Position bias too: picking a favourite from a short sequence, people strongly favoured the first wine (Mantonakis et al. 2009).

Medicine: reading the same films again

The medical re-run shows a radiologist films they read before, after a gap, mixed with others, and counts changed verdicts. Disagreeing with yourself caps how well Dr Gale and Dr Hart can agree.

Umpires: what the last call did

After calling a strike, umpires are less likely to call the next pitch a strike, the gambler's-fallacy pattern also found in asylum judges and loan officers (Chen, Moskowitz and Shue 2016). Something other than the pitch moves the call.

5. Put error bars on everything

Every number from steps 2 to 4 comes from a few hundred items at most, so its confidence interval is wide. The casebook's own numbers:

human–human κ
0.49
95% CI 0.32–0.66, n = 100
judge sensitivity
0.80
48/60 · Wilson 0.68–0.88
judge specificity
0.85
119/140 · Wilson 0.78–0.90

On 100 items, human agreement could be anywhere from “fair” to “substantial”; a judge at 0.45 or at 0.60 cannot be told apart from the humans. The sensitivity interval is wider than the specificity one because it rests on 60 YES items, not 140 NO: width depends on the count in that class, not the total. The two rates use Wilson intervals (appendix).

Rule of thumb (normal approximation, sample size): near 0.8 the 95% half-width is about 1.96 × √(0.16 / n): ±0.11 at n = 50, ±0.08 at 100, ±0.04 at 400. Sensitivity to ±0.05 takes about 250 YES items, some 800 labels at a 30% YES rate; ±0.10 takes about 60. Hamel aims for about 100 per failure mode.

Try thisLoad the sensitivity preset, then drag the items slider: the bar narrows only as the square root of the count. Set the error bar you can live with and read off how many labels it costs.

The same goes for comparing two systems later: report each score with its interval (Igor's step 6), or you cannot tell whether B beat A or got lucky.

Hiring: the small-sample interviewer

After 30 loops, an interviewer's hire rate has an interval wider than the gap between “tough” and “soft”. Calling Ana harsh on that is reading noise.

Reviews: one manager, eight reports

A manager who rated three of eight reports “exceeds” is not detectably more generous than one who rated two. Treating that as real is ranking noise.

Wine: one flight is not a verdict on the judge

One inconsistent triplicate in one flight could be an unreliable judge or one bad glass. Hodgson ran four years with 65–70 judges a year for a reason.

Medicine: few cancers per reader

Cancers are rare in screening, so one reader's year yields a handful of confirmed positives, and a sensitivity built on a handful has a very wide interval.

Umpires: thousands of calls

Thousands of pitches a season make an umpire's overall interval narrow. The noise moves to the slices: one umpire's low-edge strikes in one month is a small sample again.

6. Route on confidence: the judge takes the clear cases

A judge need not decide everything. With confidence routing it returns a score, and two thresholds split the work: above the top one it passes, below the bottom one it fails, and the middle goes to a person or a stronger, costlier grader. Igor's recipe has the same tiers among human reviewers: frontline takes the clear calls, the confusing ones go up.

Routing works only if the score is calibrated: items scored 0.9 should be right nine times in ten. A confidently wrong judge routes nothing and keeps its errors. On a synthetic identity-matching benchmark, Hightouch let a small model settle most cases and sent those it scored 30–90% to Claude Opus: 22% of items, 98.8% accuracy against Opus alone at 99.0%, $0.88 instead of $3.86 per thousand. A small model that was more accurate alone but worse calibrated fell to 91.6% as a hybrid on a harder set; the first held 95.3% (Igor's AI testing). To route, pick for calibration, not only accuracy; size the middle band on dev to what people can read, and check the auto-settled bands on test.

Hiring: the debrief spends its time on the split loops

Unanimous loops get a short debrief; the time goes to mixed ones. A hiring committee is a router: clear calls pass, borderline candidates get the expensive attention.

Reviews: the meeting discusses the boundary

A calibration meeting does not re-argue the solid “meets”. It spends its hour on people near a boundary: the uncertain middle.

Wine: the panel talks about the splits

When Elodie and Femi both score a wine far from the medal line, the score stands. When they straddle it, the panel discusses the glass. Discussion is the expensive tier, spent near the cut.

Medicine: second opinions for the hard films

Clearly normal and clearly suspicious films need no debate. Second readers and consensus review earn their cost on the films in between.

Umpires: challenge the close ones

Baseball's ball-strike challenge system, used from 2026 (FanGraphs), leaves the umpire every call and lets players send one to tracking. The challenged calls tend to be the close ones, sent to the stronger grader.

7. Keep auditing

A test passed in March is a claim about March. Three habits keep validation alive (Igor's step 9):

  • Re-label a sample on a schedule. Weekly or monthly, people label a fresh random sample of live items from every routing band; recompute sensitivity and specificity. New failures join the gold set.
  • Drift alarms. Watch numbers that need no labels: the YES rate, the share in the uncertain band, the flip rate. A sudden change means something moved, before you know what.
  • Re-validate on every change. A new model, prompt or rubric is a new judge: re-run the test set and bias checks before its scores replace the old ones, and tag scores with the judge version.

It cuts the other way too: change the grader and the graded adapt. That is Goodhart's law; once a team hill-climbs on your judge's scores, the audit sample is the only thing still measuring what you meant.

Hiring: checking interviews against outcomes

The hiring audit is slow and partial: compare interview scores with performance a year later, the way industrial psychologists validate selection (Schmidt and Hunter 1998). It never sees the rejected candidates: auditing only one routing band.

Reviews: every cycle is a re-calibration

Reviews re-run calibration every cycle because managers, teams and habits drift. A manager whose share of “exceeds” jumps between cycles is a drift alarm, whatever the reason.

Wine: the study that kept running

Hodgson's replicate pours ran four competitions, 2005 to 2008, testing 65–70 judges every year (Hodgson 2008). Testing judges once and trusting them forever is validating March.

Medicine: the audit is the law

US law (MQSA) makes every mammography facility audit outcomes: follow up positive assessments against pathology and analyse at least every 12 months, per physician and for the group, at least positive predictive value, cancer detection rate and recall rate (21 CFR 900.12(f)). It centres on the recalls; the misses are harder to see, like auditing only the YES band.

Umpires: change the grader, change the calls

When MLB shrank the grading buffer for 2025, umpires called fewer edge strikes (CBS Sports), and accuracy hit a record 92.83% that season (FanGraphs). The judged adjusted to the grader within a season, as a team optimising against your LLM judge will.

Carry this away. Qualify an LLM judge like a new human one: people and policy first, a held-out test, bias checks and error bars before anyone quotes a number, people on the uncertain middle, and back to the gold set whenever model, prompt or traffic change.

What you can say now

  • Policy and human labels come before the judge prompt; first labels are drafts.
  • Human–human agreement on the same items is the bar; compare like with like.
  • A judge is a classifier: few-shot from train, tune on dev, test once, report sensitivity and specificity, critique before verdict, pin the model.
  • Position swaps, re-runs and padded pairs catch the best-known biases.
  • At 100–200 items intervals are wide (kappa 0.32–0.66, sensitivity 0.68–0.88); width depends on the count per class, and halving it takes four times the items.
  • Confidence routing lets the judge settle the clear cases, but only if its scores are calibrated.
  • Auditing never stops: sample all bands, watch label-free drift signals, treat any model or prompt change as a new judge.
