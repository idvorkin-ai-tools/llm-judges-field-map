Three ways to ask: score one, compare two, check against a reference

The same judge gives different kinds of answer depending on the question it is handed. Each question format has a human ancestor, and each breaks in its own way.
TL;DR — There are three ways to put a question to a judge. Score one: give it one output and a scale, get back a number. That is cheap and absolute, but every judge uses the scale a little differently, and the scale drifts when the judge changes. Compare two: give it two outputs, get back which is better. That is steadier, because it needs no private scale, but it says only “better than”, never “good”, it must be run in both orders, and turning many verdicts into a ranking needs a model such as Bradley–Terry. Check against a reference: give it one output plus a known-good answer. That fixes the judge's worst weakness, problems it cannot solve itself, but it pulls the judge toward the reference and can mark a correct answer down for being different. Pick the format by what you need to decide, not by habit.

Before a judge can be good or bad, someone has to decide what to ask it. “Rate this reply from 1 to 5”, “which of these two replies is better?” and “does this reply reach the same answer as this one?” are different measurements, and they fail differently. The paper that set the vocabulary for LLM judges, Zheng et al. 2023, named all three: single-answer grading, pairwise comparison and reference-guided grading. People have used all three for as long as they have judged anything.

The three formats side by side

In the LLM version the three formats differ only in the prompt. Single-answer grading (also called pointwise or absolute grading) shows the judge one response, a rubric and a scale, and asks for a score. Pairwise comparison shows two responses to the same prompt and asks which is better, usually allowing a tie. Reference-guided grading shows one response plus a reference answer and asks the judge to grade the response with the reference in view. They can be combined: a pairwise judge can be given a reference too, and Zheng et al. did exactly that for maths.

The choice matters because each format makes the judge do a different mental task. Scoring asks the judge to hold an internal ruler steady across thousands of items. Comparing asks only for a difference between two things in front of it. Checking asks it to measure distance from a target someone else supplied. Most of what goes wrong with each format follows from that.

Three questions in hiring

An interviewer fills in a scorecard after each interview: a 1–5 on each competency, judged on its own. At the debrief, the panel often switches format without saying so: “between our two finalists, who do we want?” And for some roles the candidate does a work-sample task that is marked against an answer key. Same people, three different instruments.

Three questions in reviews

A manager writes an absolute rating for each employee against the rating definitions. Some organisations then force a relative view, stack ranking the team from top to bottom. Calibration committees often add a third: lay an employee's half-year next to an exemplar packet for each level and ask which it most resembles.

Three questions at the tasting table

A competition judge writes a score for each wine on a 20- or 100-point sheet. When two wines are close, judges often taste them side by side to decide. And a judge assessing whether a wine is typical of its region tastes it with a benchmark of the style in mind, explicitly or not.

Three questions in the reading room

A radiologist assigns a BI-RADS category to a screening mammogram on its own. Much of the real work is comparison: put this year's image next to last year's and look for anything new. And for unusual findings the reader checks a reference atlas of known appearances.

Three questions behind the plate

An umpire's call is single-answer grading by nature: one pitch, one verdict, no second pitch to compare it with. The comparison format hardly exists in umpiring. The reference format arrived with pitch tracking: the call can be checked against the measured location of the ball.

Score one: every judge has its own ruler

Single-answer grading is the default for good reasons. It costs one judge call per output. It gives an absolute number, so a new system can be scored without re-running the old ones. It fits naturally into dashboards and thresholds: “ship if the mean score is above 4.2”. MT-Bench itself uses it as the default mode: its released code recommends single-answer grading, with GPT-4 scoring each turn on a 1–10 scale (FastChat llm_judge README), and the paper reports that GPT-4's single-answer grades match both its own pairwise verdicts and human experts well, sometimes slightly worse than pairwise mode.

The weakness is that a scale lives partly inside the judge. The rubric says what a 4 means, but the judge still has to map what it sees onto those words, and two judges map differently. One is lenient and gives 4s where the other gives 3s. One avoids the ends of the scale and puts everything in the middle. One uses the full range. Each can be perfectly consistent with itself and still disagree with the other on most items, purely because their rulers are offset.

For an LLM judge the “two judges” are often the same model on two days. Change the judge model, or move to a new version of the same one, and the whole distribution of scores can slide while the outputs stay the same. Zheng et al. flag exactly this as the main cost of the format:

absolute scores are likely to fluctuate more than relative pairwise results if the judge model changes.— Zheng et al. 2023, §3.1

So a mean score is only comparable to another mean score from the same judge, the same prompt and the same rubric. Changing any of them and comparing to last quarter's number is like comparing this year's interview scores with a panel that has all-new interviewersthis cycle's ratings with a cycle under a different set of managersscores from two competitions with different juriesrecall rates from two radiology groups without auditing eitherstrikeout rates from two seasons with different umpire crews. The practical fixes are the ones human systems already use: anchored rubrics with worked examples (unit 02), a fixed set of anchor items scored again whenever the judge changes, and re-scoring the baseline system with the new judge rather than reusing its old score.

Scale use in hiring

Structured interviews put anchored example answers next to each score precisely because untrained interviewers drift: one panel's “strong yes” is another's “lean yes”. The debrief that compares Ana's and Ben's scorecards is a scale-alignment exercise as much as a decision.

Scale use in reviews

Leniency and central tendency are the classic rating errors in performance appraisal: Carla rates everyone a notch high, Dev puts everyone at “meets”. Ratings also inflate over cycles when nothing pushes back, which is the review world's version of judge drift. The calibration committee is the pushback.

Scale use at the tasting table

At the 1976 Judgment of Paris the judges scored each wine out of 20 with no shared grading framework, so each judge's ranking was built on a private scale (Judgment of Paris). Decades later, Robert Hodgson's replicate pours found that only about 10% of judges at a major competition scored the same wine within a single medal group, and about 10% on occasion scored the same wine anywhere from bronze to gold (Hodgson 2008). That is a ruler that moves within one judge, in one sitting.

Scale use in the reading room

BI-RADS exists to fix the categories: 1 negative, 2 benign, 3 probably benign, 4 suspicious, 5 highly suggestive of malignancy, each tied to an action (BI-RADS). Even so, radiologists reading the same films disagree substantially (Elmore et al. 1994 measured the variability).

Scale use behind the plate

The rulebook defines one strike zone, but each umpire calls a slightly different one, especially at the edges. Economists have used pitch tracking to measure individual umpires' zones call by call (Parsons et al. 2011). The zone is a scale with one cut point, and each umpire places it a little differently.

Compare two: steadier, but only relative

Pairwise comparison drops the private ruler. The judge does not need to know what a 4 is; it needs only to see which of two things is better, which is the easier perceptual task. Chatbot Arena chose the format for that reason. Its designers wanted many untrained people to contribute votes, and a shared scale across all of them was not realistic:

it is difficult to set a consistent grading rubric across different people. Hence, we adopt a pairwise comparison mechanism— Chiang et al. 2024, Chatbot Arena

The same reasoning applies to a model judge. Asked “which is better?”, two judges with offset rulers can still agree, because an offset that moves both outputs up a notch does not change their order. That is why pairwise results tend to be more stable across judge changes, and why A/B decisions (“is the new prompt better than the old one?”) are usually best asked directly as pairwise questions. Igor's evals recipe sets up its statistics the same way: two systems, the same items, and a judgement on each.

The format has four costs.

  • It is only relative. “A beats B” is true when both are excellent and when both are terrible. A pairwise eval can say the new system wins 70% of the time and still hide that both systems fail the policy. If the question is “is this good enough to ship?”, pairwise alone cannot answer it.
  • Order matters. Judges, human and model, tend to favour one position. Zheng et al.'s fix is conservative: ask twice with the order swapped, count a win only if the same answer wins both times, and call it a tie otherwise. Unit 06 covers position bias in detail; for this unit, the rule is that every pairwise verdict costs two calls.
  • Ties need a policy. Zheng et al.'s judge prompt allows an explicit tie, and Arena voters have two extra buttons, “tie” and “both are bad” (Chiang et al. 2024). Whether ties are dropped, split as half a win each, or modelled directly changes the final numbers, so report which choice you made.
  • It scales badly. With n systems there are n(n−1)/2 pairs, and Zheng et al. list that quadratic growth as the format's main practical limit. Comparing everything against one fixed baseline keeps the cost linear, at the price of every result being relative to that baseline.
Pairwise in hiring

Choosing between two finalists for one opening is easier than scoring each in the abstract, and panels converge faster on it. But it answers only “which one?”. If both finalists are weak, the comparison still produces a winner, which is why many hiring processes keep a bar: the best of a weak slate can still be a no hire.

Pairwise in reviews

Stack ranking makes managers order their team instead of rating each person against a standard. It removes the lenient-manager problem, because Carla and Dev can both only produce an order. It also guarantees a bottom, even on a team where everyone exceeds expectations: the relative-only cost in its sharpest form.

Pairwise at the tasting table

Two glasses side by side make small differences easy to taste that a score sheet would blur. Order and fatigue still matter: which glass comes first can shift the preference, a problem unit 06 returns to. And a side-by-side says which wine is better, not whether either deserves a medal.

Pairwise in the reading room

Comparing with the prior year's image is the radiologist's most powerful pairwise move: a new density is far easier to see against last year's picture than to judge on its own. BI-RADS even has a category for this, 0, “incomplete”, which can mean the reader needs prior images for comparison before committing (BI-RADS).

Pairwise behind the plate

Umpiring has almost no pairwise format: no one asks which of two pitches was more of a strike. That is itself a lesson. When a measured reference exists, comparing two items is wasted effort; check each one against the measurement instead.

From many verdicts to one ranking

Pairwise verdicts are not yet a leaderboard. With four systems and ten verdicts per pair, the raw data is a table of win counts, and it can contain cycles: B beats C, C beats D, and D beats B. Something has to turn that into one ordering, with a sense of how far apart the entries are.

The standard tool is the Bradley–Terry model: each contender has a hidden strength, and the chance that A beats B depends only on the ratio of their strengths. Fit the strengths that make the observed win counts most likely and you get a ranking, gaps between entries, and predicted win rates for pairs that were never compared. Bradley and Terry published it in 1952, though Zermelo had studied the same model in the 1920s (Bradley & Terry 1952; Bradley–Terry model). Thurstone's 1927 law of comparative judgment is the older psychology version of the same idea, turning “which is heavier?” judgements into a scale (Thurstone 1927).

Elo ratings, invented by Arpad Elo for chess and adopted by the US Chess Federation in 1960 (Elo rating system), use the same kind of win-probability curve but update after every game, so recent games count more and the order of games changes the result. That suits chess players, whose strength changes. It suits a fixed model version less well. Chatbot Arena started with Elo, found the online ratings noisy and order-dependent, and in December 2023 moved to fitting Bradley–Terry by maximum likelihood on all votes at once, on the grounds that a model's performance does not change between games (LMSYS, Dec 2023). The 2024 Arena paper describes the platform at over 240,000 crowd votes, with the models' names hidden until after each vote (Chiang et al. 2024).

Two cautions carry over to any home-grown leaderboard. First, a rating is only as certain as the number of verdicts behind it; Arena reports confidence intervals for this reason, and the figure shows how quickly close entries blur. Second, the ranking inherits every bias of the individual verdicts. If the judge prefers longer answers, the Bradley–Terry fit will faithfully rank the longest system first.

Check against a reference: help, and a pull

Reference-guided grading gives the judge something it may not have: the right answer. It matters most where the judge would otherwise have to solve the problem itself. Zheng et al. found GPT-4 could misjudge basic maths questions it was able to solve, accepting wrong answers; even when told to solve the problem first, it often repeated the mistake in the answer it was grading. Their fix was to have the judge solve each question independently first, then show that solution as a reference in the grading prompt. On their small test of 10 maths questions, the judge's failure rate fell from 70% to 15%. In the released MT-Bench code, maths, reasoning and coding questions are the categories graded with a reference answer (FastChat common.py).

Note what the reference was: the judge model's own separate answer, not a human key. A reference is only as good as whoever wrote it, and an error in it becomes an error in every grade that leans on it.

The bigger risk is the pull. Shown a reference, a judge tends to grade similarity to the reference rather than quality. Three problems follow:

  • Valid alternatives lose. A different proof, a correct answer in another form, or a better solution the reference author did not think of looks “wrong” because it does not match. Open-ended tasks, where many answers are good, suffer most.
  • Surface matching. An answer that copies the reference's wording but gets the substance wrong can score well. That is anchoring in the Tversky and Kahneman sense (1974): the reference sets the starting point and the judge does not adjust far enough from it.
  • Reference coverage. Where no reference exists, the judge silently falls back to its own view. Mixing reference-graded and unreferenced items in one score blends two different instruments.

The usual mitigation is in the rubric: tell the judge the reference is one correct answer among possibly many, that the task is to check correctness of the result, not resemblance, and give it an example of a correct answer that differs from the reference. Then test it: put a few known-correct alternative answers in the validation set and check they pass (unit 08).

A reference in hiring

A work-sample test marked against an answer key is reference-guided grading. Its known failure is the candidate who solved the problem a different, valid way and loses points because the key only lists one route. Good keys list several acceptable solutions and tell the grader what matters.

A reference in reviews

Exemplar packets show what work at each level looks like. They help managers align, and they also anchor: work that looks like the exemplar is rated up, work that is strong in a different way is rated down. And the exemplar is itself a committee's consensus, not an outside truth; a reference never adds information the committee did not have.

A reference at the tasting table

A benchmark wine sets what “typical of the style” means. Judged against it, an excellent but unusual wine can lose for being atypical. The benchmark is a panel's idea of the style, not a measurement of quality, so the reference here is consensus dressed as a key.

A reference in the reading room

A reference atlas of known appearances helps a reader name an unusual finding. The anchoring risk is real here too: a finding that resembles the atlas picture of something benign can be read as benign. Unlike wine or reviews, medicine can later check the read against biopsy, which is what keeps the reference honest.

A reference behind the plate

Pitch tracking is the strongest reference in this course: a measured position against a rulebook zone. Major League Baseball made it a challenge system in 2026: the umpire calls, and a team can challenge the call against the tracking. In the 2026 regular season about 54% of challenged calls were overturned (Automated Ball-Strike System). That is not the umpires' error rate: teams challenge only the calls they already think are wrong.

Two rules of thumb sit under the table. Ask the question you actually need answered: if the decision is “ship the new prompt or not”, ask the judge to compare; if it is “does this reply meet the bar”, ask it to score against the policy. And when a checkable answer exists, check it directly and skip the judge: exact match, a unit test, or a parser beats any of the three formats. A reference-guided judge is for the answers that are right but cannot be matched by string.

The formats also combine well. A common pattern is a pointwise pass-or-fail against the policy for every output, plus a pairwise comparison between the old and new system on the items both pass. The first answers “is it good enough”, the second “is it better”. That is a hiring bar plus a choice between the candidates who clear it.That is a rating against level expectations plus a comparison for promotion slots.That is a medal threshold plus a side-by-side for best in show.That is a BI-RADS category plus the comparison with the prior image.That is the live call plus a challenge against the tracking for the close ones.

Carry this away. The format is part of the measurement. Scoring asks the judge to hold a private ruler steady, so scores are absolute but drift and differ between judges. Comparing needs no ruler, so it is steadier, but it is only relative and has to be run in both orders. Checking against a reference fixes problems the judge cannot solve itself, but pulls it toward the reference and can punish correct answers that look different. Pick the one whose answer is the decision you need to make.

What you can say now

  • Single-answer, pairwise and reference-guided grading are three different instruments, named as such by Zheng et al. 2023; each has a centuries-old human counterpart.
  • Absolute scores depend on how each judge uses the scale, and they shift when the judge model or prompt changes; compare scores only within one judge setup, and re-score baselines after a change.
  • Pairwise verdicts are steadier because offsets cancel, but they say nothing about whether either answer is good, they need position swapping, and ties need a stated rule.
  • Many pairwise verdicts become a ranking through Bradley–Terry; Chatbot Arena moved from online Elo to a Bradley–Terry fit because model strength does not change between votes.
  • A reference helps most where the judge cannot reliably solve the task itself (Zheng et al.'s 10-question maths test: 70% to 15% failure), and its main risk is anchoring: correct alternatives get marked down.
