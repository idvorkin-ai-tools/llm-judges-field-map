Three ways to ask: score one, compare two, check against a reference

The same judge gives different kinds of answer depending on the question. Each format has a human ancestor, and each breaks in its own way.
TL;DR — Three ways to ask a judge. Score one: one output and a scale, back comes a number; cheap and absolute, but every judge uses the scale differently, and it drifts when the judge changes. Compare two: two outputs, back comes the better; steadier because there is no private scale, but it says only “better”, never “good”, must run in both orders, and needs a model such as Bradley–Terry to become a ranking. Check against a reference: one output plus a known-good answer; it fixes problems the judge cannot solve itself, but pulls it toward the reference and can punish a correct answer for being different. Pick the format by the decision you need.

“Rate this reply 1 to 5”, “which of these two is better?” and “does this reach the same answer as this one?” are different measurements that fail differently. Zheng et al. 2023, which set the vocabulary for LLM judges, named them single-answer grading, pairwise comparison and reference-guided grading. People have used all three for as long as they have judged anything.

The three formats side by side

For an LLM the formats differ only in the prompt. Single-answer grading (pointwise, absolute) shows one response, a rubric and a scale, and asks for a score. Pairwise comparison shows two responses to the same prompt and asks which is better, usually allowing a tie. Reference-guided grading shows one response plus a reference answer. They combine: Zheng et al. gave a pairwise judge a reference for maths.

Each makes the judge do a different task. Scoring: hold an internal ruler steady across thousands of items. Comparing: see a difference between two things in front of it. Checking: measure distance from someone else's target. Most of what goes wrong follows from that.

Three questions in hiring

An interviewer scores each competency 1–5 on its own. At the debrief the panel silently switches: “between our two finalists, who do we want?” Some roles add a work sample marked against a key. Same people, three instruments.

Three questions in reviews

A manager rates each employee against the definitions. Some organisations then stack rank the team. Calibration adds a third: which level's exemplar packet does this half-year most resemble?

Three questions at the tasting table

A judge scores each wine on a 20- or 100-point sheet, tastes close pairs side by side, and judges typicity against a benchmark of the style, explicitly or not.

Three questions in the reading room

A radiologist assigns a BI-RADS category on its own, compares this year's image with last year's for anything new, and checks unusual findings against a reference atlas.

Three questions behind the plate

An umpire's call is single-answer grading: one pitch, one verdict. Comparison hardly exists in umpiring; the reference format arrived with pitch tracking.

Score one: every judge has its own ruler

Single-answer grading is the default for good reasons: one call per output, an absolute number (a new system is scored without re-running old ones), and a natural fit for dashboards (“ship if the mean is above 4.2”). MT-Bench's released code recommends it, with GPT-4 scoring each turn 1–10 (FastChat llm_judge README), and the paper reports those grades match both its pairwise verdicts and human experts well, sometimes slightly worse than pairwise.

The weakness: a scale lives partly inside the judge. The rubric says what a 4 means, but each judge maps what it sees onto those words differently. One is lenient, one hugs the middle, one uses the full range. Each can be self-consistent and still disagree with the others on most items, purely because their rulers are offset.

For an LLM the “two judges” are often one model on two days: change the judge or its version and the score distribution can slide while the outputs stay put. Zheng et al. flag this as the format's main cost:

absolute scores are likely to fluctuate more than relative pairwise results if the judge model changes.— Zheng et al. 2023, §3.1

So a mean score compares only with one from the same judge, prompt and rubric. Changing any of them and comparing to last quarter is like comparing this year's interview scores with a panel that has all-new interviewersthis cycle's ratings with a cycle under a different set of managersscores from two competitions with different juriesrecall rates from two radiology groups without auditing eitherstrikeout rates from two seasons with different umpire crews. The fixes are the human ones: anchored rubrics (unit 02), a fixed set of anchor items re-scored whenever the judge changes, and re-scoring the baseline with the new judge.

Scale use in hiring

Structured interviews anchor each score because untrained interviewers drift: one panel's “strong yes” is another's “lean yes”. Comparing Ana's and Ben's scorecards is scale alignment as much as a decision.

Scale use in reviews

Leniency and central tendency are the classic appraisal errors: Carla rates everyone a notch high, Dev puts everyone at “meets”. Ratings also inflate over cycles, the review world's judge drift; calibration is the pushback.

Scale use at the tasting table

At the 1976 Judgment of Paris judges scored out of 20 with no shared framework, each on a private scale (Judgment of Paris). Hodgson's replicate pours later found only about 10% of judges at a major competition kept the same wine within one medal group, and about 10% on occasion scored it anywhere from bronze to gold (Hodgson 2008): a ruler moving within one judge, in one sitting.

Scale use in the reading room

BI-RADS fixes the categories, each tied to an action (BI-RADS), yet radiologists reading the same films still disagree substantially (Elmore et al. 1994).

Scale use behind the plate

The rulebook defines one zone, but each umpire calls a slightly different one, especially at the edges; pitch tracking measures each umpire's zone call by call (Parsons et al. 2011).

Compare two: steadier, but only relative

Pairwise comparison drops the private ruler: the judge only has to see which of two things is better, the easier perceptual task. Chatbot Arena chose it because many untrained voters could not share a scale:

it is difficult to set a consistent grading rubric across different people. Hence, we adopt a pairwise comparison mechanism— Chiang et al. 2024, Chatbot Arena

Two judges with offset rulers can still agree on “which is better?”, because an offset that lifts both outputs a notch leaves their order alone. So pairwise results are steadier across judge changes, and A/B decisions (“is the new prompt better?”) are best asked directly. Igor's evals recipe sets up its statistics this way: two systems, the same items, a judgement on each. It has four costs.

  • Only relative. “A beats B” holds when both are excellent and when both are terrible. A 70% win rate can hide that both systems fail the policy, so pairwise alone cannot answer “good enough to ship?”.
  • Order matters. Judges favour one position (position bias, unit 06). Zheng et al. ask twice with the order swapped and count a win only if it wins both times, else a tie: every verdict costs two calls.
  • Ties need a policy. Zheng et al.'s prompt allows a tie; Arena voters also get “tie” and “both are bad” (Chiang et al. 2024). Dropping ties, splitting them or modelling them changes the numbers; say which.
  • Scales badly. n systems make n(n−1)/2 pairs, the format's main practical limit for Zheng et al. A fixed baseline keeps cost linear, with every result relative to it.
Pairwise in hiring

Choosing between two finalists is easier than scoring each, and panels converge faster. But two weak finalists still produce a winner, so many processes keep a bar: the best of a weak slate can be a no hire.

Pairwise in reviews

Stack ranking removes the lenient-manager problem, since Carla and Dev can only produce an order. It also guarantees a bottom on a team where everyone exceeds: the relative-only cost at its sharpest.

Pairwise at the tasting table

Side by side, small differences a score sheet would blur become easy to taste. Order and fatigue still shift the preference (unit 06), and the result says which wine is better, not whether either deserves a medal.

Pairwise in the reading room

The prior-year comparison is the radiologist's strongest pairwise move: a new density is far easier to see against last year's picture. BI-RADS 0, “incomplete”, can mean the reader needs prior images before committing (BI-RADS).

Pairwise behind the plate

No one asks which of two pitches was more of a strike. The lesson: when a measured reference exists, check each item against it instead of comparing.

From many verdicts to one ranking

Pairwise verdicts are not yet a leaderboard. Four systems with ten verdicts per pair give a table of win counts that can contain cycles (B beats C, C beats D, D beats B). Something has to turn it into one ordering with gaps.

The standard tool is the Bradley–Terry model: each contender has a hidden strength, and the chance A beats B depends only on the ratio of their strengths. Fit the strengths that make the observed wins most likely and you get a ranking, gaps, and predicted win rates for pairs never compared. Bradley and Terry published it in 1952; Zermelo studied it in the 1920s (Bradley & Terry 1952; Bradley–Terry model). Thurstone's 1927 law of comparative judgment is the psychology version, turning “which is heavier?” into a scale (Thurstone 1927).

Try thisDrag one pair's result and watch every rating move; give D a clean sweep, or no wins at all, and the fit has no answer.

Elo ratings, from chess (adopted by the US Chess Federation in 1960; Elo rating system), use the same win-probability curve but update after every game, so recent games count more and game order changes the result. That suits players whose strength changes, not a fixed model version. Chatbot Arena found its online Elo noisy and order-dependent, and in December 2023 moved to a maximum-likelihood Bradley–Terry fit on all votes at once, since a model does not change between games (LMSYS, Dec 2023). The 2024 Arena paper reports over 240,000 crowd votes, model names hidden until after each vote (Chiang et al. 2024).

Two cautions for any home-grown leaderboard. A rating is only as certain as the verdicts behind it; Arena reports confidence intervals, and the figure shows how fast close entries blur. And the ranking inherits every bias of the verdicts: if the judge prefers longer answers, the fit faithfully ranks the longest system first.

Check against a reference: help, and a pull

A reference gives the judge the right answer, which matters most where it would otherwise have to solve the problem itself. Zheng et al. found GPT-4 accepting wrong answers to maths questions it could solve; told to solve first, it often copied the mistake from the answer it was grading. Their fix: have the judge solve each question separately, then show that solution as the reference. On 10 maths questions the failure rate fell from 70% to 15%. MT-Bench's code grades maths, reasoning and coding with a reference (FastChat common.py).

That reference was the judge's own separate answer, not a human key. A reference is only as good as its writer, and its errors become every grade's errors.

The bigger risk is the pull: shown a reference, a judge grades similarity rather than quality.

  • Valid alternatives lose. A different proof, another correct form, or a better solution looks “wrong” for not matching; open-ended tasks suffer most.
  • Surface matching. Copying the reference's wording with the substance wrong can score well: anchoring in Tversky and Kahneman's sense (1974), where the judge does not adjust far enough from the starting point.
  • Coverage. Without a reference the judge silently falls back on its own view; mixing referenced and unreferenced items blends two instruments in one score.

Mitigate in the rubric: the reference is one correct answer among possibly many, check correctness not resemblance, and here is a correct answer that differs. Then test it with known-correct alternatives in the validation set (unit 08).

A reference in hiring

A work sample marked against a key fails the candidate who solved it a different valid way. Good keys list several solutions and say what matters.

A reference in reviews

Exemplar packets align managers and anchor them: work like the exemplar is rated up, work strong in another way down. And the exemplar is a committee's consensus, adding nothing the committee did not know.

A reference at the tasting table

A benchmark wine defines “typical”, so an excellent unusual wine can lose for being atypical. The benchmark is a panel's idea of the style: consensus dressed as a key.

A reference in the reading room

An atlas helps name an unusual finding, and anchors too: one resembling a benign picture can be read as benign. Unlike wine or reviews, biopsy later keeps the reference honest.

A reference behind the plate

Pitch tracking is the strongest reference here: a measured position against a rulebook zone. MLB made it a challenge system in 2026, and about 54% of challenged calls that regular season were overturned (Automated Ball-Strike System). That is not the error rate: teams challenge only calls they already think are wrong.

Ask the question you need answered: “ship the new prompt?” is a comparison; “does this meet the bar?” is a score against the policy. And when a checkable answer exists (exact match, a unit test, a parser), skip the judge. A reference-guided judge is for answers that are right but cannot be matched by string.

The formats combine: a pointwise pass/fail against the policy for every output, plus a pairwise comparison of old and new on items both pass, answers “good enough?” and “better?”. That is a hiring bar plus a choice between the candidates who clear it.That is a rating against level expectations plus a comparison for promotion slots.That is a medal threshold plus a side-by-side for best in show.That is a BI-RADS category plus the comparison with the prior image.That is the live call plus a challenge against the tracking for the close ones.

Carry this away. The format is part of the measurement. Scores are absolute but rest on a private ruler that drifts and differs between judges. Comparisons need no ruler but are only relative and must run in both orders. A reference fixes problems the judge cannot solve but pulls it toward one answer. Pick the format whose answer is your decision.

What you can say now

  • Single-answer, pairwise and reference-guided grading are three instruments (Zheng et al. 2023), each with an old human counterpart.
  • Absolute scores depend on each judge's use of the scale and shift when the judge or prompt changes; compare within one setup and re-score baselines.
  • Pairwise verdicts are steadier because offsets cancel, but say nothing about whether either answer is good, need order swapping, and need a tie rule.
  • Bradley–Terry turns pairwise verdicts into a ranking; Chatbot Arena left online Elo for it because model strength does not change between votes.
  • A reference helps where the judge cannot solve the task (70% to 15% failure on Zheng et al.'s 10 maths questions); its risk is anchoring on one correct answer.
