Nothing here is new

An LLM judge is a rater. People have rated things without an answer key for as long as there have been exams, juries and tastings, and they have already met every problem in this course.
TL;DR — An LLM judge reads an output and gives it a grade, usually because there is no answer key to check against and too many outputs for people to read. That makes it a measuring instrument, and every instrument has to answer two questions: do two readings of the same thing agree (reliability), and do the readings track what is really there (validity)? Hiring panels, managers, wine juries, radiologists and umpires have been answering those two questions for a century or more, with names and statistics this course borrows directly. The one thing to remember: before asking whether the model is a good judge, ask where the truth it is judged against comes from, because that varies by domain far more than the model does.

Like almost everything in machine learning, the LLM judge is an old idea in a new machine. The pipeline is new: a prompt, a rubric, a model call, a score. The problem is not. It is the problem of anyone asked to say whether something is good when no answer key exists, and the human institutions that do that job have spent a long time finding out how it breaks.

Pick a domain from the Metaphor menu at the top of the page. Every amber phrase and every boxed example on the site switches to that domain, while the LLM side of the explanation stays put. The domains are chosen because each one has a well-studied version of the judging problem, and each has its own idea of where truth comes from.

In the interview room

An interviewer spends an hour with a candidate and writes “hire” or “no hire”. No answer key exists for “will this person do well here”. The interviewer is the instrument, and the company finds out how good an instrument they were only a year later, and only for the people it hired.

In the review cycle

A manager reads six months of an employee's work and writes “exceeds” or “meets”. No answer key exists for that either. The manager is the instrument, and the calibration meeting where several managers compare ratings is how the organisation checks it.

At the tasting table

A competition judge tastes a wine poured blind into a numbered glass and writes a score that decides whether it gets a medal. Nobody can look up whether the wine deserved one. The judge's palate is the instrument, and pouring the same wine twice in one flight is how a competition finds out whether the instrument is steady.

In the reading room

A radiologist reads a screening mammogram and decides whether to call the patient back. The image does not come labelled. The radiologist is the instrument, and medicine is the one domain here that can usually check it later: a biopsy, or a year of follow-up, says whether cancer was there.

Behind the plate

An umpire watches a pitch the batter did not swing at and calls “strike” or “ball” in a fraction of a second. The rulebook defines the zone; the umpire's eyes are the instrument. Since pitch-tracking cameras arrived, every call can be checked against a measured position.

Why anyone uses a judge

A judge is what people reach for when checking against an answer key is impossible. Some tasks have one: arithmetic, a unit test, an exact-match label. Most of what people want from a language model does not. “Was this summary faithful?”, “was this reply helpful and kind?” and “did the agent do what the user meant?” are questions with better and worse answers but no single right one. Igor's evals recipe puts the cost plainly: once the grader cannot be a function, it becomes a second source of noise, and the grade can move even when the output did not.

Three things push teams from human raters to a model. Volume: a prompt change can be tested on thousands of outputs, and nobody reads thousands of outputs twice a day. Speed: a model returns a grade in seconds, which turns evaluation into something that runs on every change rather than once a quarter. Cost: the work humans did per item drops to near zero. None of those three says anything about whether the grades are right. They say only that the judge is cheap enough to be tempting, which is why the rest of this course is about checking it.

The same trade has been made before. A hiring process that once relied on one founder's gut adds structured interviews when it has to hire hundreds of people. A wine competition with thousands of entries cannot have one famous palate taste them all, so it seats panels. Breast screening programmes read millions of images, so they write reporting standards and audit their readers. Each time, the move from one trusted expert to many cheaper judges forced the same questions this course asks of a model.

They met every problem first

Almost every failure listed in units 06 and 07 was found, named and partly fixed by people judging people or things. A few examples, one per domain:

  • Hiding who wrote it. China's imperial examinations made scripts anonymous at the palace examination in 992, and later had clerks recopy every answer so that examiners could not recognise a candidate's handwriting (Imperial examination). That is a defence against the judge favouring a known author, which unit 07 calls self-preference when the author is the judge's own model family.
  • One good trait colouring the rest. In 1920 Edward Thorndike asked commanding officers to rate their soldiers on separate qualities, and found the ratings moved together far more than the qualities plausibly did. He called it a “constant error”; it is now called the halo effect (Thorndike 1920).
  • Two readers, two answers. In 1947 Jacob Yerushalmy studied how different readers of the same chest X-rays disagreed, and in doing so introduced the terms sensitivity and specificity (Yerushalmy 1947). Three years later W. J. Youden proposed a single index for rating a diagnostic test, the J statistic in unit 05 (Youden 1950).
  • Agreement beyond chance. Jacob Cohen published kappa in 1960 because psychologists coding the same behaviour needed to know how much of their agreement was luck (Cohen 1960). Unit 04 uses it unchanged.
  • The same wine, a different score. Robert Hodgson had the California State Fair pour some wines three times within a judge's flight, and measured how often a judge gave the same wine the same score (Hodgson 2008). That is the self-consistency check any LLM judge should get.
  • Checking the call against a machine. Tennis has let players challenge line calls against Hawk-Eye ball tracking since 2006 (Hawk-Eye), and economists have studied millions of called pitches, comparing parks where a camera system monitored umpires with parks where it did not, to show that umpire bias shrinks when the calls are being checked (Parsons et al. 2011).

The model-era literature knows this. The paper that made “LLM-as-a-judge” a standard term, Zheng et al. 2023, measured its judges the way the older fields measure people: how often the model agrees with human experts compared with how often the experts agree with each other, and which systematic biases (position, length, self-preference) push its verdicts around. What follows treats it the same way.

A judge is an instrument

Treat the judge as a measuring device and two separate questions fall out. Reliability: if the same item is graded twice, by the same judge or by two judges, do the grades agree? Validity: do the grades track the property they claim to measure? The four targets show why the two must be checked separately. A judge can be perfectly consistent and consistently wrong, and a judge can be right on average while any single grade is close to a coin flip.

The two questions map onto the middle of this course. Unit 04 measures reliability: how often judges agree, and how much of that agreement chance alone would produce. Unit 05 measures validity, by comparing the judge to whatever stands in for the truth. Most practical trouble with LLM judges is a reliability number reported as if it were a validity number: “the judge agrees with GPT-whatever 90% of the time” says the two are consistent with each other, and nothing about whether either is right.

Reliability caps validity. A judge whose grade for the same item wanders from one reading to the next cannot track the truth any better than it tracks itself. That is why every serious evaluation starts with the cheapest reliability check there is: grade the same items twice and count how often the verdict changes.

Where truth comes from

The five domains differ less in how their judges fail than in what the judges can be checked against. That difference sets how far anyone can trust a judge, so it is worth fixing early.

Truth in hiring

Hiring has a truth, but it arrives late and only for some people: how a hire performs over the following year. Candidates who were rejected never generate an outcome, so the judge's “no hire” calls can never be checked. Industrial psychologists have long measured interview methods this way, by correlating ratings with later job performance (Schmidt and Hunter 1998), and the “later performance” in such studies is often itself a supervisor's rating.

Truth in performance reviews

Performance reviews have almost no outside truth. The rating is the measurement; there is nothing further down to check it against except other people's ratings. That is why the review world leans so hard on calibration meetings: when no answer key exists, agreement among informed judges is the best stand-in available.

Truth in wine judging

Wine has no outside truth about quality at all. A lab can confirm a fault such as cork taint, but nothing confirms that a wine deserved gold. Competitions can only test judges against each other and against themselves, which is why the replicate-pour studies matter so much in this domain.

Truth in medicine

Medicine is the domain with the strongest reference standard: biopsy for the women who are called back, and later diagnosis or a clean year of follow-up for the women who are not. It is still imperfect, because the reference has its own error and not every case gets the same check. Diagnostic-test statistics were born here for that reason.

Truth behind the plate

Umpiring now has a measured truth. A camera system records where every pitch crossed the plate, and the strike zone is defined in the rulebook, so each call can be scored right or wrong after the game. The judge is still human because the call has to be made live and the system's own error is small but not zero.

LLM tasks spread along the same axis. Where there is a checkable answer, use it: code that passes tests needs no judge. Where the property is faithfulness to a source, a careful human can usually settle it, so the judge can be checked against people the way a radiologist is checked against biopsy. Where the property is tone, helpfulness or taste, the task sits at the wine end. The only available truth is the judgement of people who have agreed on a policy, which is why unit 02 starts with writing that policy down.

Carry this away. An LLM judge is a rater, and the oldest rater questions apply to it unchanged: does it agree with itself, does it agree with other judges, and does it agree with the truth, wherever that comes from? Ask the third question last, because its answer depends on a source of truth that many tasks do not have.

What you can say now

  • An LLM judge is used where no answer key exists and the volume is too high for people; cheapness is the reason to use one, not evidence that it works.
  • Reliability (agreeing with itself and with other judges) and validity (agreeing with the truth) are separate checks, and reliability caps validity.
  • Anonymous scripts, halo error, reader variability, kappa, Youden's J and replicate pours all predate language models by decades or centuries; this course borrows them as they are.
  • Domains differ most in where truth comes from: measured for pitch calls, biopsy for mammograms, late and partial for hiring, and nothing beyond other judges for wine quality and performance ratings. LLM tasks spread along the same range.
