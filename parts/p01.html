THE FRAME · UNIT 01 OF 09why judges · reliability and validity · where truth comes from

Nothing here is new

An LLM judge is a rater. People have graded without an answer key for as long as there have been exams, juries and tastings, and they met every problem in this course first.
TL;DR — An LLM judge grades outputs when there is no answer key and too many outputs for people to read. That makes it a measuring instrument with two questions to answer: do two readings of the same thing agree (reliability), and do they track what is really there (validity)? Hiring panels, managers, wine juries, radiologists and umpires have answered both for a century, and this course borrows their names and statistics. Before asking whether the model is a good judge, ask where the truth it is checked against comes from: that varies by domain far more than the model does.

The pipeline is new: a prompt, a rubric, a model call, a score. The problem is old: say whether something is good when no answer key exists. The institutions that do that job have spent a long time learning how it breaks.

Pick a domain from the Metaphor menu at the top. Every amber phrase and boxed example on the site switches to it; the LLM side stays put. Each domain has a well-studied version of the judging problem and its own idea of where truth comes from.

In the interview room

An interviewer writes “hire” or “no hire” after an hour. No key exists for “will this person do well here”; the company learns how good the call was a year later, and only for people it hired.

In the review cycle

A manager reads six months of work and writes “exceeds” or “meets”. No key exists; the calibration meeting, where managers compare ratings, is the check.

At the tasting table

A judge tastes a wine poured blind and scores it for a medal nobody can look up. Pouring the same wine twice in a flight tests whether the palate is steady.

In the reading room

A radiologist reads a screening mammogram and decides whether to call the patient back. Medicine can usually check later: a biopsy or a year of follow-up says whether cancer was there.

Behind the plate

An umpire calls “strike” or “ball” in a fraction of a second against the rulebook zone. Pitch-tracking cameras now check every call against a measured position.

Why anyone uses a judge

A judge is what people reach for when there is no answer key. Arithmetic, a unit test or an exact-match label has one; most of what people want from a language model does not. “Was this summary faithful?” and “did the agent do what the user meant?” have better and worse answers but no single right one. Igor's evals recipe names the cost: once the grader cannot be a function, it is a second source of noise, and the grade can move when the output did not.

Teams move to a model for volume, speed and cost. None of the three says the grades are right, only that the judge is tempting, so the rest of this course is about checking it. The trade is old: structured interviews replace a founder's gut at hundreds of hires, wine competitions seat panels, screening programmes audit their readers. Each move from one trusted expert to many cheaper judges raised the questions this course asks of a model.

They met every problem first

Almost every failure in units 06 and 07 was found, named and partly fixed by people judging people or things:

  • Hiding who wrote it. China's imperial examinations made scripts anonymous at the palace examination in 992, and later had clerks recopy every answer so examiners could not recognise handwriting (Imperial examination). It defends against favouring a known author, which unit 07 calls self-preference when the author is the judge's own model family.
  • One good trait colouring the rest. In 1920 Edward Thorndike had officers rate soldiers on separate qualities and found the ratings moved together far more than the qualities plausibly did. He called it a “constant error”; it is now the halo effect (Thorndike 1920).
  • Two readers, two answers. In 1947 Jacob Yerushalmy studied how readers of the same chest X-rays disagreed, and introduced the terms sensitivity and specificity (Yerushalmy 1947). In 1950 W. J. Youden proposed one index for rating a diagnostic test, the J statistic of unit 05 (Youden 1950).
  • Agreement beyond chance. Jacob Cohen published kappa in 1960 so psychologists coding the same behaviour could tell how much of their agreement was luck (Cohen 1960). Unit 04 uses it unchanged.
  • The same wine, a different score. Robert Hodgson had the California State Fair pour some wines three times in a judge's flight and counted how often the judge gave the same score (Hodgson 2008): the self-consistency check every LLM judge should get.
  • Checking the call against a machine. Tennis has let players challenge line calls against Hawk-Eye since 2006 (Hawk-Eye). Economists comparing millions of pitches in parks with and without camera monitoring found umpire bias shrinks when calls are checked (Parsons et al. 2011).

The paper that made “LLM-as-a-judge” a standard term, Zheng et al. 2023, measured its judges the same way: model-to-expert agreement against expert-to-expert agreement, plus the biases that push verdicts around.

A judge is an instrument

Treat the judge as a measuring device and two questions fall out. Reliability: graded twice, by the same judge or by two, do the grades agree? Validity: do the grades track the property they claim to measure? A judge can be consistently wrong, or right on average while any single grade is close to a coin flip, so the two are checked separately.

Unit 04 measures reliability, unit 05 validity. Much practical trouble is one reported as the other: “the judge agrees with GPT-whatever 90% of the time” says the two are consistent, not that either is right.

Reliability caps validity: a judge whose grade wanders between readings cannot track the truth better than it tracks itself. So start with the cheapest check: grade the same items twice and count how often the verdict changes.

Where truth comes from

The five domains differ less in how their judges fail than in what the judges can be checked against, and that sets how far anyone can trust a judge.

Truth in hiring

Hiring's truth arrives late and only for hires; “no hire” calls are never checked. Interview methods are validated against later job performance (Schmidt and Hunter 1998), which is often itself a supervisor's rating.

Truth in performance reviews

Performance reviews have almost no outside truth: the rating is the measurement, checkable only against other people's ratings. Hence calibration meetings: with no answer key, agreement among informed judges is the best stand-in.

Truth in wine judging

Wine has no outside truth about quality. A lab can confirm cork taint, but nothing confirms a wine deserved gold. Competitions can only test judges against each other and themselves, which is why replicate pours matter so much here.

Truth in medicine

Medicine has the strongest reference: biopsy for women called back, follow-up for the rest. It is imperfect (its own error, uneven checks), which is why diagnostic-test statistics were born here.

Truth behind the plate

Umpiring has a measured truth: cameras record where each pitch crossed the rulebook zone, so every call can be scored afterwards. The judge stays human because the call is live and the cameras' error is small but not zero.

LLM tasks spread along the same axis. Code that passes tests needs no judge. Faithfulness to a source can usually be settled by a careful human, so the judge is checked against people the way a radiologist is checked against biopsy. Tone, helpfulness and taste sit at the wine end: the only truth is people who agreed on a policy, which is why unit 02 starts by writing it down.

Carry this away. An LLM judge is a rater, and the old rater questions apply unchanged: does it agree with itself, with other judges, and with the truth? Ask the third last; it needs a source of truth many tasks lack.

What you can say now

  • An LLM judge is used where no answer key exists and volume is too high for people; cheapness is the reason to use one, not evidence it works.
  • Reliability (agreeing with itself and other judges) and validity (agreeing with the truth) are separate checks, and reliability caps validity.
  • Domains differ most in where truth comes from: measured for pitch calls, biopsy for mammograms, late and partial for hiring, other judges only for wine and reviews. LLM tasks spread along the same range.
