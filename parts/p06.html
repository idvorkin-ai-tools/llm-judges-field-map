How judges go wrong I: what they are shown

The same item, presented differently, gets a different grade. Every one of these biases lives in the input, not in the judge, which is good news: the input is the part you control.
TL;DR — An LLM judge's verdict moves with things that should not matter: which answer is shown first, what it was graded after, how long it is, what number sat in the prompt before it, and how confidently it is formatted. Human judges move with exactly the same things, and their institutions found the fixes first: rotate the order, pour blind, write your score before you see anyone else's, and judge the content separately from the packaging. For LLM judges the fixes are cheap and mechanical: run both orders and count a flip as a tie, grade each item in a fresh call, control for length, keep prior scores out of the prompt, and score accuracy on its own line. The one number to remember: in the casebook, 38 of 100 pairwise verdicts change when the two answers swap places.

A judge is supposed to respond to the item. Everything on this page is a way the judge responds to something else: the slot the item sits in, its neighbours, its size, a number shown beside it, its clothes. These are presentation biases. They are the easier half of judge failure, because you can change the presentation without changing the judge. Unit 07 takes the harder half, the biases that come from who is judging.

In the interview room

Two finalists for one opening, interviewed back to back by the same interviewer. Nobody chose the order on purpose; the first one was free on Tuesday morning. The interviewer's notes on the second candidate are written in the shadow of the first, and the debrief will be argued on those notes.

In the review cycle

A manager writes eight reviews in a week, in whatever order the inbox serves them. The employee reviewed right after the team's star reads a little flatter than they would have on Monday morning, and last cycle's rating sits at the top of the form before the manager writes a word.

At the tasting table

A competition judge works through a flight of thirty glasses. The wine in glass 1 is tasted on a fresh palate; the wine in glass 22 follows three big, oaky reds. Same scoring sheet, same judge, different conditions, and only the glass number knows it.

In the reading room

A radiologist works down a worklist of screening mammograms. Each one is read beside last year's image and the referral note, and after the previous reader's report. The worklist order, the prior and the notes all reach the reader before the pixels do.

Behind the plate

An umpire calls a borderline pitch right after calling the last two pitches strikes, with a catcher who has learned to catch the ball so it looks a little closer to the zone than it was. The pitch's measured location is fixed. Everything around it is not.

Position: whichever is shown first

Give an LLM judge two answers and ask which is better. Then swap them and ask again. A judge that responds only to the answers gives the same winner both times. Real judges often do not. Wang et al. 2023 showed how far this goes: with ChatGPT as the judge, they could make Vicuna-13B beat ChatGPT on 66 of 80 questions just by choosing the order. They call the share of examples whose verdict changes under a swap the conflict rate; On the Vicuna-versus-ChatGPT comparison GPT-4 conflicted on 46.3% of questions and ChatGPT on 82.5%. The two judges even leaned different ways, GPT-4 towards the first answer and ChatGPT towards the second.

Zheng et al. 2023 ran the cleanest version of the test, and a deliberately hard one: two near-identical answers to each MT-bench question, sampled from the same model, judged in both orders. GPT-4 gave the same verdict both ways in 65% of cases, favoured whichever came first in 30% and whichever came second in 5%. Claude-v1 was consistent in only 23.8% of cases and favoured the first answer in 75%. Wang et al. found the other half of the story: the closer the two answers are in quality, the more likely the swap flips the verdict. Position bias is a tie-breaker. It does the most damage exactly where the comparison is hardest, which is where an eval most needs to be right.

Run the casebook numbers forward to see why this matters for a leaderboard. Suppose a script always puts model X's answer first. Of the 100 pairs, the 62 consistent ones go to whichever answer is better. Of the 38 that flip, X collects 30 and the rival 8, purely from the slot. X's record is inflated by a 22-pair swing that has nothing to do with quality, and nobody looking at the final win rate can see it.

The fix is the oldest one there is: run both orders. Zheng et al. use the conservative rule, a win only when the same answer wins in both orders and a tie otherwise, which turns the casebook into 62 decided pairs and 38 ties. Wang et al.'s balanced position calibration averages the scores across the two orders, which keeps more signal when the judge emits scores rather than a bare choice. Either way, report the inconsistency rate next to the win rate. It is the cheapest reliability number an LLM judge can give you, and a 38% swap rate tells you more about how far to trust the judge than its agreement with any other model does.

The two finalists

A hiring committee that sees two finalists in a fixed order is running the single-order test. Panels that split the interviewers, so half of them see candidate A first and half see B first, are running the swap. If the debrief splits along that line, the panel has learned the comparison was close and that order, not the candidates, decided several votes.

The stack rank

A calibration meeting that discusses employees in the same order every cycle hands the first names a fresh room and the last ones a tired one. Rotating the order of teams and names from cycle to cycle is the swap. Comparing two close employees twice, once with each one first, is the swap done by hand.

Two glasses side by side

Many competitions pour flights in a randomised order, and sensory scientists balance which glass sits on the left in a paired test. A judge who prefers the left glass on both pours of the same pair has shown a position preference, not a palate.

Comparing two images

Pairwise judging in medicine is mostly the comparison with last year's image, so order shows up as which image the reader looks at first. Reader studies that compare two methods typically counterbalance the reading order for that reason: half the readers see method A first, half see method B.

Comparing two calls

Umpires rarely compare two items side by side, so position in the pairwise sense barely exists behind the plate. The order problem umpires do have is sequence, the call that came just before, which the next section but one covers with the best evidence on this site.

Order: the first glass and the last case

Position is about two items in one prompt. Order is the same effect stretched over a list: which item is judged first, fifth or last. Psychologists have studied it for a century as the serial-position effect, and the cleanest demonstration for this course comes from wine. Mantonakis, Rodero, Lesschaeve and Hastie 2009 had people taste a sequence of two to five wines, one at a time, and pick their favourite at the end. Every glass held the same wine. Even so, the first wine had a large advantage, and tasters who knew a lot about wine also showed a recency effect in the longer sequences, favouring the last glass. With nothing to choose between, the slot did the choosing.

LLM judges meet order in two places. The first is batch grading: putting ten answers in one prompt and asking for ten scores. Every answer now has a position, and the answers early in the list are graded with a different context from the ones late in it. The second is the order of anything else in the prompt, such as few-shot examples or rubric items. The position results above give good reason to expect order effects in both places; how large they are for a given judge is something to measure, not assume.

The fix is to make order random and then make it a variable. Shuffle the items for every run, record each item's position, and check whether score depends on position. If it does, the judge has an order effect, and shuffling turns it from a bias into noise that averages out across items. Better still, grade one item per call, so that each item has no neighbours at all.

The interview loop

An onsite loop has an order too: the first interviewer meets a fresh candidate, the last meets a tired one, and the hiring manager often reads the feedback in the order it was submitted. Randomising which interviewer goes first, and having everyone submit before reading anyone else's, removes the order from the decision rather than hoping it washes out.

The review queue

A manager who writes reviews in alphabetical order gives the same employees the first, fresh slot every year. Shuffling the queue each cycle does not remove the order effect from any single review, but it stops the same people paying for it every time.

The flight

The Mantonakis result is why competitions randomise the order of wines within a flight and between judges. A wine that always lands in glass 1 would carry the primacy advantage into its medal count; randomising spreads that advantage evenly across entries, where it cancels.

The worklist

A radiologist's worklist is ordered by something: arrival time, urgency, the system's default sort. The first and last cases of a long session are read in different states from the middle ones. Audit studies that want to measure a reader, rather than the worklist, present cases in a random order for each reader.

The game

An umpire cannot shuffle the pitches. The order is the game. That makes umpires the best natural experiment for the next effect, where the item that came before changes the call on this one.

Sequence: after a run of yeses, a no

Order is about where an item sits. Sequence is about what came right before it. The best evidence anywhere for a sequence effect, and the best multi-domain source on this site, is Chen, Moskowitz and Shue 2016. They studied three sets of professional judges whose cases arrive in an order unrelated to their merits, and found the same pattern in all three: after deciding one way, the judge is less likely to decide the same way next time, whatever the next case is.

  • US asylum judges (150,357 decisions, 1985 to 2013): 0.5 percentage points less likely to grant asylum after granting the previous case, rising to 3.3 points for judges with moderate grant rates hearing similar cases on the same day, and 5.5 points after two grants in a row.
  • Loan officers in a field experiment in India, where the true quality of each loan file was known: 8 points less likely to approve after approving the previous file under flat pay, and much less affected when they were paid for accuracy.
  • Major League umpires (pitch-tracking data, so the true location of each pitch is controlled for): 1.5 points less likely to call a strike after calling the previous pitch a strike, with the effect more than doubling for pitches near the edge of the zone.

The authors read this as the gambler's fallacy: people expect runs of the same outcome to be rarer than chance makes them, so after three grants a fourth feels wrong. They say plainly that they cannot fully rule out a contrast effect instead, where a case simply looks worse next to a good one. For an eval the mechanism matters less than the shape, which is the same either way: the effect is strongest on borderline items, after streaks, with less experienced judges and when accuracy is not rewarded.

Does an LLM judge do this? A judge called fresh for every item cannot: it has no memory of its last verdict. A judge that grades a batch in one prompt, or works through items in one long conversation with its earlier verdicts in context, has the same structure as an asylum docket. Whether models show the human pattern, its reverse or nothing is an empirical question this page has no verified study for, so treat it as a hypothesis to test rather than a known result. The test is easy. Log the judge's verdicts in the order it produced them and check whether verdict n predicts verdict n+1 once you control for the item, which is exactly the regression Chen and colleagues ran.

The fix is stateless grading: one item per call, no previous verdicts in the prompt, items shuffled. The paper adds a lesson for the human side of the loop. The loan officers paid for accuracy showed a much smaller effect. When people label the gold set that the judge is checked against, give them feedback on their accuracy, and they will be a steadier reference.

The screening stack

A recruiter who has just advanced three candidates in a row may feel the fourth needs to clear a higher bar, though the paper did not study recruiters. Structured scorecards help because they make the reviewer score this candidate against fixed anchors rather than against a running sense of how many yeses is too many.

The run of strong reviews

A manager who has written three “exceeds” ratings in a row may feel pressure to write a “meets” next. Some of that is a real quota or a guideline about rating distributions, which is a separate problem; some of it is the sense that four in a row cannot be right. Writing each review against the level definitions, not against the previous review, is the fix.

After the big wines

A delicate wine poured after three powerful ones can taste thin; one poured after three faults can taste better than it is. That is the contrast reading of the same pattern. Competitions group wines by style within a flight partly so each glass is tasted next to its peers.

After the recalls

A reader who has just called back three patients may hesitate before a fourth recall. Screening programmes audit each reader's recall rate over hundreds of cases precisely so that the right rate is a property of the reader's whole caseload, not something felt from the last few films.

After two strikes

This is the measured case. Holding the tracked location of the pitch fixed, umpires call fewer strikes right after calling strikes, and the effect is biggest at the edge of the zone, where the call is hardest. The pitch did not change; the umpire's expectation of a run did.

Length: more words, more points

Verbosity bias is the judge preferring a longer answer when the extra length adds nothing. Zheng et al. built a sharp test for it, the “repetitive list” attack. They took 23 MT-bench answers that contained a numbered list, had GPT-4 rephrase the list without adding any information, and stuck the rephrased copy on the front, so a five-item list became ten items saying the same five things. Then they asked judges which answer was better. Claude-v1 and GPT-3.5 preferred the padded answer 91.3% of the time; GPT-4 only 8.7%. Stronger judges resist it better, but nobody should assume their judge is the strong kind without running the test.

Length bias also shows up at benchmark scale. AlpacaEval, which uses an LLM judge to compare a model's answers with a baseline's, was known to favour models that write longer outputs. Dubois et al. 2024 fixed it with a regression: model the judge's preference as a function of quality and the length difference, then ask what the preference would be if the two answers were the same length. Length control raised the benchmark's Spearman correlation with Chatbot Arena rankings from 0.94 to 0.98, and made the score harder to game by being wordy.

Humans are not immune; they are the reason the problem is old. Chatbot Arena's style control is a fix applied to human voters, not a model: when the Arena team added answer length and markdown counts to its rating model, the leaderboard reordered, and length was the dominant style factor. The people voting in the Arena reward length too.

Three fixes, from cheapest to strongest. Put length in the rubric: tell the judge that repetition and padding cost points and that a complete short answer beats a long one. Run the repetitive-list attack as a canary on your own judge: pad twenty answers without adding information and count how often the score goes up. And when you compare systems, control for length the way Dubois et al. did, by regressing the judge's verdict on the length difference and reporting the length-controlled number alongside the raw one.

The long answer

A candidate who talks for eight minutes on a question that needed three sounds thorough, and an interviewer taking notes ends up with more notes. A scorecard with anchored answers, where each level says what a good answer contains, lets the interviewer check content rather than volume.

The thick packet

An employee who submits a twelve-page self-review next to a colleague's two pages has made more claims, not necessarily more impact. Managers who cap the self-review length, or score against a list of outcomes rather than against the packet, are doing length control by hand.

The big wine

Wine has no word count, but tasters often say its analogue is intensity: in a flight of thirty, a powerful, concentrated wine stands out and a subtle one can be lost. Whether judges systematically reward size is argued more than measured; grouping wines by style within a flight is the usual defence.

The long report

A radiologist does not grade reports by length, but a long, hedged prior report can read as more careful than a short, decisive one. Structured reporting with BI-RADS categories forces the conclusion into one fixed slot, which is length control for the part that drives the decision.

No word count

A pitch has no length, so umpiring has no verbosity bias in the literal sense. Its presentation cousin is how the ball is caught, which belongs to the style section below.

Anchors: the number in the room

Anchoring is the judgement drifting towards a number seen just before it, even an irrelevant one. In Tversky and Kahneman 1974, people watched a wheel of fortune land on 10 or 65, then estimated the share of African countries in the United Nations. The median estimates were 25% and 45%. The wheel carried no information, and it still moved the answer by twenty points.

An LLM judge's prompt is full of candidate anchors: a reference answer, a previous judge's score, a scale description that mentions a typical grade, an earlier draft's verdict. Some of these are there on purpose and help. Zheng et al. found that GPT-4, asked to grade maths answers, was often misled by the answers themselves, making the same mistake as the answer it was grading. Their reference-guided judge, which first solves the problem independently and then grades against its own solution, cut the failure rate on their test from 14 of 20 to 3 of 20. That is anchoring used deliberately: give the judge a correct anchor and it follows it. The danger is the same mechanism with a wrong anchor, a reference answer nobody checked or a prior score that was itself noise.

The fix is blinding. Keep previous scores, other judges' verdicts and the item's source out of the prompt unless the task is explicitly to review them. When you do want a reference answer, verify it first, or have the judge produce its own before it sees the answer being graded, as Zheng et al. did. And when you measure agreement between two judges in unit 04's sense, make sure neither saw the other's grade, or the agreement you measure is partly copying.

Feedback before the debrief

An interviewer who reads the recruiter's screen notes, or a colleague's “strong hire”, before writing their own feedback is grading against an anchor. Processes that lock each interviewer's written feedback before anyone can see the others' exist for this reason; the debrief starts from independent readings.

Last cycle's rating

A review form that shows last cycle's rating at the top hands the manager an anchor before the first sentence. Ratings tend to stick near it, which is comfortable for everyone and hides real change in either direction. Writing the assessment first and looking at history after is the blinded version.

The label and the panel

Pouring blind is anchoring control: the label, the price and the producer's reputation are the strongest anchors a wine has, so competitions remove them. Panels then have each judge write a score before discussion, so the first confident voice at the table does not set everyone else's number.

The prior image and the prior report

Reading a mammogram next to last year's image is standard practice and genuinely useful, because change over time is a signal. The prior conclusion is a different thing: a previous reader's “benign” is an anchor, not evidence. Double reading, where the second reader does not see the first reader's call, keeps the two readings independent.

The call on the field

Replay review in many sports starts from the call already made, and overturns it only when the video clearly shows it was wrong. That deliberately builds the anchor into the rule, trading some accuracy on close calls for fewer reversals. It is a fine design choice, as long as everyone knows the review is not an independent second reading.

Style: dressed like a good answer

The last presentation bias is the broadest: the judge rewarding how an answer looks rather than what it says. Headers, bold text, tidy bullet lists, a confident tone, an authoritative register. Wu and Aji 2023 built a set of deliberately flawed answers, had crowd workers, experts and LLM judges compare them, and found answers with factual errors rated more favourably than answers that were too short or contained grammatical errors. A wrong answer in good clothes beat a right answer in bad ones. The Chatbot Arena style-control work above found the same thing at scale for human voters, with markdown headers, bold and lists as second-order effects behind length.

Style bias is hard to remove because style is not irrelevant. A well-organised answer is often a better answer. The problem is when style stands in for substance in a single overall grade. Wu and Aji's fix is to stop merging everything into one score: grade accuracy, helpfulness and language as separate dimensions, and the judge's accuracy grade improved. The practical versions: put factual accuracy on its own rubric line with its own evidence requirement; for comparisons, strip or normalise formatting before judging when format is not what you are testing; and use regression-style control, as the Arena does, when you report a leaderboard.

The polished candidate

A candidate with a confident delivery, the right vocabulary and a good story structure can carry a thin answer a long way. Scoring each competency separately, against anchored descriptions of what a strong answer contains, makes the interviewer write down the substance before the overall impression.

The well-presented packet

An employee who writes crisp summaries with charts and headings presents better than one whose impact is buried in a plain list. Calibration committees that ask “what changed because of this work?” for each claim are separating substance from presentation, one claim at a time.

The bottle

Wine judging solved the biggest style cue centuries before LLMs existed: the wine is poured into an identical numbered glass, so the bottle shape, the label design and the price cannot be seen. What is left is the wine's own style, which is a legitimate thing to judge as long as the scoring sheet says how much it counts.

The referral note

The style cue in screening is authority: a referral note that sounds worried, or a prior report from a senior colleague, arrives with weight the image may not support. Standardised BI-RADS categories and audit of each reader's results against biopsy keep the decision tied to the image.

The frame

Catchers practise “framing”: receiving a borderline pitch so it looks closer to the zone than it was. Chen and colleagues note framing as something that shifts called-strike rates. The pitch's tracked location is the substance; the catch is the style, and the umpire's call rewards some of it.

Every row has the same shape. Change the presentation while holding the item fixed, and see whether the grade moves. If it does, the judge is responding to the presentation, and the fix is either to remove that part of the presentation or to randomise it so it averages out. This is the perturbation habit that Igor's grade-the-grader step asks for, and it costs a few hundred extra judge calls, which is nothing next to shipping an eval whose verdicts depend on the order of a list.

Carry this away. Presentation biases live in the prompt, not the model, so they are the cheap ones to fix. For every judge, test each one directly: swap the order, shuffle the batch, check the verdict sequence, pad an answer, add and remove a prior score, reformat the same content. Then design the pipeline so the bias cannot act: both orders, one item per call, blind to prior scores, accuracy scored on its own. Human judging got there first with randomised flights, blind pours and independent double reading.

What you can say now

  • Pairwise LLM judges change their verdict when the answers swap places, most often on close comparisons; Zheng et al. found GPT-4 consistent in 65% of hard pairs, and in the casebook 38 of 100 pairs flip, 30 of them towards the first-shown answer.
  • Running both orders and counting a flip as a tie removes position bias from the result and gives a free reliability number, the swap rate.
  • Order and sequence effects are old and measured in people: identical wines are preferred when poured first, and asylum judges, loan officers and umpires are less likely to repeat a decision right after making it. Stateless, shuffled grading removes both from an LLM judge.
  • Length and style move judge scores, human and model alike; the repetitive-list attack and a reformat test expose it, and length- or style-controlled comparisons correct for it.
  • Any number in the prompt is an anchor. A verified reference answer helps; an unverified one or a prior score turns a second reading into a partial copy of the first.
