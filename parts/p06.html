How judges go wrong I: what they are shown

The same item, shown differently, gets a different grade. These biases live in the input, which is the part you control.
TL;DR — An LLM judge's verdict moves with things that should not matter: which answer comes first, what came before it, its length, a number in the prompt, its formatting. Human judges do the same, and their fixes carry over cheaply: run both orders and count a flip as a tie, grade each item in a fresh call, control for length, keep prior scores out of the prompt, score accuracy on its own line. In the casebook, 38 of 100 pairwise verdicts change when the two answers swap places.

Each bias here is the judge responding to something other than the item: its slot, its neighbours, its size, a number beside it, its clothes. These are the easier half of judge failure, because you can change the presentation without changing the judge. Unit 07 takes the harder half: who is judging.

In the interview room

Two finalists, interviewed back to back by one interviewer, in an order nobody chose: the first was free on Tuesday morning. The notes on the second are written in the shadow of the first, and the debrief is argued on those notes.

In the review cycle

A manager writes eight reviews in a week, in inbox order. The employee reviewed right after the team's star reads a little flatter, and last cycle's rating sits at the top of the form before the manager writes a word.

At the tasting table

A judge works through a flight of thirty glasses. Glass 1 meets a fresh palate; glass 22 follows three big, oaky reds. Same sheet, same judge, different conditions.

In the reading room

A radiologist works down a worklist of screening mammograms, each read beside last year's image, the referral note and the previous reader's report. The order, the prior and the notes all arrive before the pixels do.

Behind the plate

An umpire calls a borderline pitch right after two strikes, with a catcher who catches it so it looks closer to the zone than it was. The pitch's measured location is fixed. Everything around it is not.

Position: whichever is shown first

Ask a judge which of two answers is better, then swap them and ask again. A judge that responds only to the answers picks the same winner twice. Real judges often do not. With ChatGPT as the judge, Wang et al. 2023 made Vicuna-13B beat ChatGPT on 66 of 80 questions just by choosing the order. The share of verdicts that change under a swap, their conflict rate, was 46.3% for GPT-4 and 82.5% for ChatGPT, and the two leaned opposite ways: GPT-4 to the first answer, ChatGPT to the second.

Zheng et al. 2023 ran a deliberately hard version: two near-identical answers to each MT-bench question, from the same model, judged in both orders. GPT-4 was consistent in 65% of cases, favoured the first answer in 30% and the second in 5%. Claude-v1 was consistent in only 23.8% and favoured the first in 75%. Wang et al. add that the closer the two answers, the likelier the flip. Position bias is a tie-breaker: it does the most damage where the comparison is hardest.

Now suppose a leaderboard script always puts model X's answer first. The 62 consistent pairs go to whichever answer is better. Of the 38 that flip, X collects 30 and the rival 8, purely from the slot: a 22-pair swing that has nothing to do with quality and is invisible in the final win rate.

The fix is the oldest one: run both orders. Zheng et al.'s conservative rule counts a win only when the same answer wins both ways and a tie otherwise, turning the casebook into 62 decided pairs and 38 ties. Wang et al.'s balanced position calibration averages scores across the two orders, keeping more signal when the judge emits scores. Either way, report the swap rate beside the win rate: it is the cheapest reliability number a judge gives you.

The two finalists

A committee that meets two finalists in a fixed order is running the single-order test. Splitting the interviewers, half seeing A first and half B, is the swap. If the debrief splits along that line, order, not the candidates, decided several votes.

The stack rank

A calibration meeting that discusses employees in the same order every cycle gives the first names a fresh room and the last a tired one. Rotating the order each cycle is the swap; comparing two close employees twice, each one first once, is the swap by hand.

Two glasses side by side

Many competitions randomise the pour order, and sensory scientists balance which glass sits on the left in a paired test. A judge who prefers the left glass on both pours of the same pair has shown a position preference, not a palate.

Comparing two images

Pairwise judging in medicine is mostly the comparison with last year's image. Reader studies comparing two methods counterbalance the reading order: half the readers see method A first, half method B.

Comparing two calls

Umpires rarely compare two items side by side, so pairwise position barely exists behind the plate. Their order problem is sequence, the call just before, covered two sections down with the best evidence on this site.

Order: the first glass and the last case

Position is two items in one prompt. Order is the same effect over a list: which item is judged first, fifth or last. Psychologists call it the serial-position effect, and the cleanest demonstration is wine. Mantonakis, Rodero, Lesschaeve and Hastie 2009 had people taste two to five wines one at a time and pick a favourite. Every glass held the same wine. The first still had a large advantage, and knowledgeable tasters also favoured the last glass in longer sequences. The slot chose.

LLM judges meet order in batch grading (ten answers in one prompt, ten scores out, each graded in a different context) and in the order of anything else in the prompt, such as few-shot examples or rubric items. Expect order effects in both; measure their size for your judge rather than assume it.

The fix: shuffle the items every run, record each item's position, and check whether score depends on it. Shuffling turns an order effect from a bias into noise that averages out. Better still, grade one item per call, so no item has neighbours.

The interview loop

An onsite loop has an order: the first interviewer meets a fresh candidate, the last a tired one, and the hiring manager reads feedback in submission order. Randomising who goes first, and having everyone submit before reading anyone else's, removes order from the decision.

The review queue

Writing reviews alphabetically gives the same employees the fresh slot every year. Shuffling the queue does not remove the effect from any single review, but it stops the same people paying for it every time.

The flight

This is why competitions randomise wine order within a flight and between judges. A wine that always lands in glass 1 would carry the primacy advantage into its medal count; randomising spreads it across entries, where it cancels.

The worklist

A worklist is ordered by arrival, urgency or a default sort, and the first and last cases of a long session are read in different states from the middle. Audit studies that measure a reader present cases in a random order for each reader.

The game

An umpire cannot shuffle the pitches; the order is the game. That makes umpires the best natural experiment for the next effect.

Sequence: after a run of yeses, a no

Order is where an item sits; sequence is what came right before it. The best evidence for a sequence effect, and the best multi-domain source on this site, is Chen, Moskowitz and Shue 2016. In three sets of professional judges whose cases arrive in an order unrelated to merit, they found one pattern: after deciding one way, the judge is less likely to decide the same way next time, whatever the next case is.

  • US asylum judges (150,357 decisions, 1985 to 2013): 0.5 percentage points less likely to grant after granting the previous case; 3.3 points for moderate-grant-rate judges hearing similar cases the same day; 5.5 points after two grants in a row.
  • Loan officers in an Indian field experiment, with each file's true quality known: 8 points less likely to approve after an approval under flat pay, much less when paid for accuracy.
  • Major League umpires (pitch tracking controls for true location): 1.5 points less likely to call a strike after a strike, more than doubling near the edge of the zone.

The authors read this as the gambler's fallacy: after three grants, a fourth feels wrong. They cannot fully rule out a contrast effect, where a case looks worse next to a good one. For an eval the shape matters more than the mechanism: the effect is strongest on borderline items, after streaks, with less experienced judges, and when accuracy is not rewarded.

Does an LLM judge do this? One called fresh per item cannot; one grading a batch, or a long conversation with earlier verdicts in context, has the structure of an asylum docket. This page has no verified study of whether models show the pattern, so treat it as a hypothesis. To test it, check whether verdict n predicts verdict n+1 once you control for the item, the regression Chen and colleagues ran.

The fix is stateless grading: one item per call, no previous verdicts in the prompt, items shuffled. And for the humans who label the gold set: the loan officers paid for accuracy showed a much smaller effect, so give labellers feedback on their accuracy.

The screening stack

A recruiter who has just advanced three candidates may feel the fourth must clear a higher bar, though the paper did not study recruiters. Structured scorecards help: the reviewer scores this candidate against fixed anchors, not a running sense of how many yeses is too many.

The run of strong reviews

After three “exceeds” ratings in a row, a manager may feel pressure to write a “meets”. A real distribution guideline is a separate problem; the sense that four in a row cannot be right is this one. Write each review against the level definitions, not the previous review.

After the big wines

A delicate wine after three powerful ones can taste thin; one after three faults can taste better than it is. That is the contrast reading. Competitions group wines by style within a flight partly so each glass meets its peers.

After the recalls

A reader who has just called back three patients may hesitate before a fourth. Screening programmes audit each reader's recall rate over hundreds of cases so the right rate belongs to the whole caseload, not the last few films.

After two strikes

The measured case. Holding the tracked pitch location fixed, umpires call fewer strikes right after strikes, most of all at the edge of the zone. The pitch did not change; the umpire's expectation of a run did.

Length: more words, more points

Verbosity bias is the judge preferring a longer answer when the extra length adds nothing. Zheng et al.'s “repetitive list” attack tests it: take 23 MT-bench answers containing a numbered list, have GPT-4 rephrase the list without adding information, and put the copy on the front, so five items become ten saying the same five things. Claude-v1 and GPT-3.5 preferred the padded answer 91.3% of the time; GPT-4 only 8.7%. Run the test before assuming your judge is the strong kind.

It shows up at benchmark scale. AlpacaEval, an LLM judge comparing a model's answers with a baseline's, favoured longer outputs. Dubois et al. 2024 fixed it with a regression: model the judge's preference from quality and the length difference, then ask what it would be at equal length. Length control raised the benchmark's Spearman correlation with Chatbot Arena rankings from 0.94 to 0.98.

Humans are not immune. Chatbot Arena's style control is a fix applied to human voters: when the Arena team added answer length and markdown counts to its rating model, the leaderboard reordered, and length was the dominant style factor.

Three fixes, cheapest first. Put length in the rubric: padding costs points, and a complete short answer beats a long one. Run the repetitive-list attack as a canary: pad twenty answers without new information and count how often the score rises. And when comparing systems, control for length as Dubois et al. did, reporting the length-controlled number beside the raw one.

The long answer

A candidate who talks for eight minutes on a three-minute question sounds thorough and fills more notes. A scorecard whose levels say what a good answer contains lets the interviewer check content, not volume.

The thick packet

A twelve-page self-review beside a colleague's two pages makes more claims, not necessarily more impact. Capping self-review length, or scoring against a list of outcomes, is length control by hand.

The big wine

Wine's analogue of length is intensity: in a flight of thirty, a concentrated wine stands out and a subtle one can be lost. Whether judges systematically reward size is argued more than measured; grouping by style is the usual defence.

The long report

A long, hedged prior report can read as more careful than a short, decisive one. BI-RADS categories force the conclusion into one fixed slot, which is length control for the part that drives the decision.

No word count

A pitch has no length, so umpiring has no literal verbosity bias. Its presentation cousin is how the ball is caught, in the style section below.

Anchors: the number in the room

Anchoring is a judgement drifting towards a number seen just before it, even an irrelevant one. In Tversky and Kahneman 1974, people watched a wheel of fortune land on 10 or 65, then estimated the share of African countries in the United Nations. Median estimates: 25% and 45%. The wheel carried no information and still moved the answer twenty points.

A judge prompt is full of anchors: a reference answer, a previous score, a scale that mentions a typical grade. Some help. Zheng et al. found GPT-4 grading maths answers often made the same mistake as the answer it graded; their reference-guided judge, which solves the problem first and grades against its own solution, cut failures on their test from 14 of 20 to 3 of 20. That is anchoring used on purpose. The danger is the same mechanism with a wrong anchor: an unchecked reference, or a prior score that was itself noise.

The fix is blinding. Keep previous scores, other judges' verdicts and the item's source out of the prompt unless reviewing them is the task. Verify any reference answer, or have the judge write its own before it sees the answer being graded. And when you measure agreement between two judges as in unit 04, make sure neither saw the other's grade, or the agreement is partly copying.

Feedback before the debrief

An interviewer who reads the screen notes, or a colleague's “strong hire”, before writing feedback is grading against an anchor. Locking each interviewer's feedback before anyone sees the others' means the debrief starts from independent readings.

Last cycle's rating

A form that shows last cycle's rating at the top hands the manager an anchor before the first sentence. Ratings stick near it, hiding real change either way. Writing the assessment first and looking at history after is the blinded version.

The label and the panel

Pouring blind is anchoring control: label, price and reputation are a wine's strongest anchors, so competitions remove them. Each judge then writes a score before discussion, so the first confident voice does not set everyone's number.

The prior image and the prior report

Reading beside last year's image is useful, because change over time is a signal. The prior conclusion is different: a previous reader's “benign” is an anchor, not evidence. Double reading, where the second reader does not see the first's call, keeps the readings independent.

The call on the field

Replay review starts from the call already made and overturns it only on clear evidence. That builds the anchor into the rule, trading some accuracy on close calls for fewer reversals: fine, as long as everyone knows the review is not an independent second reading.

Style: dressed like a good answer

Style bias rewards how an answer looks, not what it says: headers, bold, tidy bullets, a confident tone. Wu and Aji 2023 had crowd workers, experts and LLM judges compare deliberately flawed answers, and answers with factual errors were rated more favourably than answers that were too short or had grammatical errors. A wrong answer in good clothes beat a right one in bad clothes. Arena's style control found the same for human voters, with headers, bold and lists as second-order effects behind length.

Style is hard to remove because it is not irrelevant: a well-organised answer is often better. The problem is style standing in for substance in one overall grade. Grading accuracy, helpfulness and language separately improved Wu and Aji's accuracy grades. In practice: give factual accuracy its own rubric line and evidence requirement, normalise formatting when format is not under test, and use regression-style control, as the Arena does, for leaderboards.

The polished candidate

Confident delivery, the right vocabulary and good story structure can carry a thin answer a long way. Scoring each competency against anchored descriptions makes the interviewer write down substance before the overall impression.

The well-presented packet

Crisp summaries with charts present better than impact buried in a plain list. Committees that ask “what changed because of this work?” for each claim separate substance from presentation one claim at a time.

The bottle

Wine solved the biggest style cue centuries ago: identical numbered glasses, so bottle, label and price are invisible. What remains is the wine's own style, legitimate to judge as long as the sheet says how much it counts.

The referral note

Screening's style cue is authority: a worried referral note or a senior colleague's prior report arrives with weight the image may not support. BI-RADS categories and auditing each reader against biopsy keep the decision tied to the image.

The frame

Catchers practise “framing”: receiving a borderline pitch so it looks closer to the zone. Chen and colleagues note framing shifts called-strike rates. The tracked location is the substance; the catch is the style, and the call rewards some of it.

Every row has one shape: change the presentation, hold the item fixed, see whether the grade moves. If it does, remove that part of the presentation or randomise it so it averages out. This is the perturbation habit Igor's grade-the-grader step asks for, and it costs a few hundred judge calls, nothing next to an eval whose verdicts depend on the order of a list.

Carry this away. Presentation biases live in the prompt, so they are the cheap ones. Test each directly (swap, shuffle, check the sequence, pad, add a prior score, reformat), then build the pipeline so they cannot act: both orders, one item per call, blind to prior scores, accuracy scored on its own.

What you can say now

  • Pairwise LLM judges flip when the answers swap places, most on close calls: GPT-4 was consistent on 65% of Zheng et al.'s hard pairs; in the casebook 38 of 100 pairs flip, 30 towards the first-shown answer.
  • Running both orders and counting a flip as a tie removes position bias and gives a free reliability number, the swap rate.
  • Order and sequence effects are measured in people: identical wines win when poured first, and asylum judges, loan officers and umpires avoid repeating a decision. Stateless, shuffled grading removes both.
  • Length and style move scores, human and model alike; the repetitive-list attack and a reformat test expose it, and length or style control corrects it.
  • Any number in the prompt is an anchor. A verified reference helps; a prior score makes the second reading a partial copy.
