How judges go wrong II: who is judging

The last unit was about what the judge is shown. This one is about the judge itself: what it likes, where its scale sits, what it defers to, how it changes over time, and what happens once people start optimising against it.
TL;DR — Some judge errors travel with the judge rather than with the item. An LLM judge can favour text that reads like its own, sit high or low on the scale, pile every score into the middle, let one strong criterion lift all the others, defer to claimed authority, and change silently when the model or prompt changes. The worst one comes later: once a model is trained or tuned to please the judge, it learns the judge's weak spots, and the judge's score stops meaning what it meant. Every one of these has a long human history, and the human fixes carry over: don't let a judge grade its own kind, anchor the scale, score criteria separately, hide who wrote it, pin the judge, and keep checking it against people the optimiser never sees. Baseball umpires supply the cleanest evidence that measuring a judge changes what the judge does.

A judge is not a neutral pipe between an output and a score. It brings tastes, a habitual place on the scale, and things it trusts that it should not. Humans have names for all of these because personnel psychologists spent the twentieth century finding them in rating forms. A model brings its own versions, and it adds one problem people rarely have: an optimiser on the other side that can try millions of variations to find what the judge likes.

The examples below switch with the Metaphor menu. Throughout, the judge is an interviewera managera competition judgea radiologista plate umpire, and the YES call is “hire”“exceeds expectations”“medal”“recall”“strike”.

Favouring its own kind

Self-preference is a judge scoring outputs from its own model higher than humans would. It was flagged early: the MT-Bench paper saw GPT-4 give itself about a 10% higher win rate than humans did, and Claude-v1 about 25%, but was careful to say its data could not settle whether this was a real bias (Zheng et al. 2023). Panickssery, Bowman and Feng 2024 ran the controlled version on news summarisation. GPT-3.5, GPT-4 and Llama 2 all favoured their own summaries over other models' and over human-written ones. All three could also tell their own text apart better than chance; GPT-4 did so 73.5% of the time against two other models and humans. When the authors fine-tuned models to get better or worse at recognising themselves, self-preference rose and fell with it, roughly linearly. The judge is not only biased towards its own output. It appears to recognise that output and prefer it because it recognises it.

The wider version is familiarity: a judge rewards the style it is used to. A judge that shares a model family with the system under test will tend to like that family's phrasing, structure and hedges, which is the common setup.

Hiring people like me

Lauren Rivera interviewed 120 people who hired for elite professional-services firms and sat in on a hiring committee. Evaluators looked for candidates who shared their leisure pursuits, experiences and self-presentation, and that similarity often outweighed concerns about how productive the candidate would be (Rivera 2012). An interviewer who rewards “reminds me of me” is doing what a model does when it rewards text that reads like its own.

Rating your own hires

A manager who argued to hire someone now rates that person's work. Every rating is also a verdict on the manager's own judgement, so the manager has a stake in the answer. The fix organisations use is the one this page recommends for models: someone without that stake reviews the rating, and calibration compares it with ratings of people the manager did not hire.

A palate trained on one style

The crude version, a judge scoring their own wine, is what blind pouring prevents. The subtle version survives blinding: a winemaker who judges will recognise the style they make, the oak regime or the ripeness level, and the wines that taste like home taste right. The blind glass hides the label, not the style, which is exactly the gap a model's self-recognition exploits.

Reading your own earlier report

A radiologist comparing today's image with a prior study they reported themselves is checking their own work. In double reading, the second reader's value comes from independence; a second reader who can see the first reader's call is closer to a rubber stamp than a second opinion. Keeping the reads independent is the medical form of not letting a judge grade its own output.

Calling for your own side

The best-measured case of a judge favouring its own kind is behind the plate. Economists found that umpires called strikes more often when they shared the pitcher's race or ethnicity, but mainly where nobody was checking (Parsons et al. 2011). That study gets its own section below, because the “where nobody was checking” part is the most useful finding on this page.

The defence is centuries old. China's imperial examinations had clerks recopy every script so that examiners could not recognise a candidate's handwriting, as unit 01 described. The LLM version of recopying: judge with a different model family from the generator, or a panel drawn from several families; and normalise surface style (formatting, punctuation, stock openers) before judging, as Panickssery et al. did. Then measure it by breaking judge-versus-human agreement down by which model wrote the output. If the judge beats the humans' scores most on its own family's outputs, that gap is the self-preference on your task.

Where the judge sits on the scale

Rating research has three names for a judge whose scale is in the wrong place. Leniency is a judge whose scores run high; severity is one whose scores run low; central tendency is one who avoids both ends and gives nearly everything the middle score. All three can coexist with good ranking: a lenient judge may order items correctly and still pass far too many. That is why unit 05 separates the ranking (the ROC curve, AUC 0.868 in the casebook) from the cut: leniency is a judge whose effective cut sits lower than the policy's.

LLM judges show all three. Central tendency is the best documented. The G-Eval authors noticed that when an LLM scores on a 1–5 scale, one digit often dominates, such as 3, which shrinks the variance and the correlation with humans (Liu et al. 2023). Their fix was to weight each possible score by the probability the model gives its token, rather than take the single digit it printed. Practitioners report the other direction too, a judge that puts nearly everything at 4 out of 5. Either way, a scale that the judge uses only one or two points of carries little more information than a coin.

The casebook already contains a mild case. In the two-rater example of unit 04, interviewer Ana says “hire” to 60% of candidates and Ben to 55%manager Carla rates 60% of her employees “exceeds” and Dev 55%judge Elodie gives medals to 60% of the wines and Femi to 55%Dr Gale recalls 60% of the patients and Dr Hart 55%umpire Ivo calls 60% of the pitches strikes and June 55%. In the confusion matrix of unit 05, the judge says YES on 34.5% of items when only 30% are truly YES: it leans slightly lenient, and that lean is a large part of why it has 21 false positives.

The easy interviewer and the hard one

Every hiring panel knows which interviewer passes almost everyone and which one rejects almost everyone. Neither is useless: each still ranks candidates. But a candidate's outcome then depends on which interviewer they drew, and a hiring committee that counts votes without knowing each interviewer's base rate is adding numbers in different units.

The generous manager

A manager who rates most of the team “exceeds” and one who rates almost nobody that way can be describing teams of the same strength. Forced distributions and calibration meetings exist mainly to correct for this: the meeting is where one manager's “exceeds” is translated into another's.

The high scorer at the table

On a panel, one judge's 90 is another's 86. Competitions that award medals by a panel's average are exposed to whoever happens to sit on the panel, which is one reason some panels discuss scores and reconcile them rather than simply averaging.

Ten radiologists, ten thresholds

When ten radiologists each read the same 150 mammograms, the share of cancer-free women they would have sent for immediate work-up ranged from 11% to 65% (Elmore et al. 1994). For the women who did have cancer the range was 74% to 96%. Same films, very different thresholds: severity and leniency, measured against biopsy and follow-up.

The pitcher's umpire and the hitter's umpire

Players talk about umpires with a big zone and umpires with a tight one. Pitch tracking turns that gossip into a number: each umpire's called-strike rate on pitches in the same locations. A big-zone umpire is a lenient judge in exactly the sense of the figure, with a cut that sits lower than the rulebook's.

The fix. First, measure the judge's YES rate (or mean score) on a set humans have labelled, and compare it with theirs; a gap is leniency or severity before anything else is wrong. Second, anchor the scale: write down what a 2 and a 4 look like with examples, which is unit 02's job. Third, consider dropping the scale. Hamel Husain's advice is to start with pass/fail judgements and a written critique, because nobody can say what separates a 3 from a 4 (Husain). A binary judge can still be lenient, but its leniency is a single number you can read off a confusion matrix and move by editing the prompt.

One good trait lifting the rest

In 1920 Edward Thorndike looked at ratings that officers gave their men on separate qualities, such as physique, intellect and leadership, and found the ratings correlated far more than the underlying qualities plausibly could. Raters were forming one general impression and letting it colour every line of the form (Thorndike 1920). That is the halo effect, and its negative twin, where one bad trait drags the rest down, is sometimes called the horns effect.

The LLM version appears whenever one call scores several criteria. A prompt that says “rate this answer for faithfulness, completeness, clarity and tone, 1–5 each” asks the model to write four numbers in sequence, each conditioned on the ones before it and on whatever overall verdict its reasoning has already reached. The likely result is four numbers that move together. This page knows of no study that pins down the size of the LLM halo, so treat it as a risk to measure rather than a measured fact. The check is easy: on a human-labelled set, compare the correlations between criterion scores from the judge with the correlations between the humans' criterion scores. If the judge's criteria correlate at 0.9 where the humans' correlate at 0.4, the rubric has collapsed into one score. A sharper test is to damage one criterion on purpose, for example by inserting a factual error into an otherwise fluent answer, and see whether the tone and clarity scores fall as well.

Scoring competencies one at a time

Structured interviews assign each interviewer specific competencies and ask for a score per question against anchored answers, then compare notes only after everyone has written their scores down. Each of those rules is a halo defence: fewer criteria per judge, evidence written before the number, and no contamination from the room.

The project that rated everything

One visible launch can lift every line of an employee's review, including lines about collaboration that the launch says nothing about. Review forms that ask for a specific example under each dimension make the halo visible: a dimension with no example of its own is probably borrowing its score from another.

The twenty-point sheet

A score sheet that splits a wine into appearance, nose, palate and finish only helps if the judge scores each part on its own evidence. A judge who has already decided “this is a gold” tends to fill in the parts to add up to it, and the sub-scores then tell the competition nothing the total did not.

The report form

A BI-RADS report records breast density and the individual findings separately from the final assessment category (BI-RADS). The structure is there so the overall call follows from the findings rather than the findings being filled in to match the call.

Status as a halo

An umpire makes one call, but something other than the pitch can still colour it. Using pitch-tracking data, Jerry Kim and Brayden King found umpires were more likely to call borderline pitches strikes for high-status pitchers, and less likely to miss true strikes from them, and that the errors changed those pitchers' results (Kim and King 2014). The pitcher's reputation, a fact about the person and not the pitch, lifted the score.

The fix. Score each criterion in its own call, with only that criterion's definition in the prompt; Igor's evals recipe defines a rubric as a per-task checklist whose items are each checked separately (step 4). If cost forces one call, require evidence under each criterion before any number, and ask for the overall score last. Prefer narrow binary checks (“does the answer cite the clause it relies on?”): a narrow question leaves less room for an impression to leak in.

Deferring to claimed authority

A judge should score the content. A judge with authority bias scores who the content claims to come from. Chen et al. 2024 tested this by adding fake references to answers and found both human and LLM judges were swayed by the look of credibility; they then used fake references and rich formatting as a prompt-based attack on LLM judges, succeeding up to 50% of the time on GPT-4. The CALM framework lists authority bias among twelve biases it measures in LLM judges (Ye et al. 2024). The same mechanism covers an output that opens with “As a board-certified physician…”, an answer full of confident citations nobody checked, and the plainest attack of all: text inside the output addressed to the judge, telling it what score to give.

Models are also trained to agree with the person they talk to, so a judge prompt that mentions “the user believes this is correct”, or passes along an earlier verdict, invites sycophancy. Anything in the judge's context that is not the output, the policy or the reference is a potential lever.

The senior referral

A candidate referred by a senior leader, or with a famous employer on the CV, walks in with a borrowed score. Some hiring processes hide referral source and pedigree from the interviewers who assess skills, for the same reason the judge prompt should not contain the generator's name.

The senior voice in calibration

In a calibration meeting, the most senior person's opinion of an employee tends to become the room's opinion. Meetings that ask each manager to state a rating before discussion, and to cite evidence rather than impressions, are guarding against authority bias.

The price on the label

In one experiment, people tasted wines they were told cost different amounts; the higher price raised how pleasant they said the wine was, and raised activity in a brain region linked to experienced pleasantness (Plassmann et al. 2008). The participants were ordinary volunteers, not competition judges, but the reason competitions pour blind is that nobody wants to find out how far a famous label moves a professional.

The note on the request

A request form that says “senior colleague thinks this is benign” is not evidence about the image. Clinical history can be legitimate information; an opinion about the answer is not. Independent double reading exists so that the second read is not just the first read with a second signature.

The star pitcher

The status finding in the halo section is also an authority finding: a well-known pitcher gets the edges of the zone on reputation. A catcher or manager arguing a call is trying to apply the same pressure live.

The fix. Strip author identity, model names, claimed credentials and prior verdicts from what the judge sees. Fence the output off as data, with the instruction that nothing inside it is addressed to the judge. Verify citations separately with a tool rather than letting the judge be impressed by them. And test for it directly: take a set of graded outputs, add a fake credential or a fake reference, and count how many verdicts flip. The flip rate is your authority bias.

The judge changes over time

Human judges drift with fatigue, mood and time of day. The most famous claim is the “hungry judge” study: Israeli parole boards granted about 65% of requests at the start of each session, falling to nearly zero before a food break and jumping back after it (Danziger, Levav and Avnaim-Pesso 2011). Treat it as contested. A letter in the same journal reported, from court data and interviews, that the order of cases was not random: the boards tried to finish one prison's cases before a break, and within a session prisoners without lawyers, who succeeded far less often, usually went last (Weinshall-Margel and Shapard 2011). Later simulations argued that scheduling could produce at least part of the pattern and that the effect's size is overestimated (Glöckner 2016). The story is a good warning about drift and a better warning about confounded orderings. Firmer evidence of fatigue comes from radiology, in the medical box below.

An LLM judge does not get tired or hungry. It drifts in other ways, and more abruptly. The provider updates the model behind a name. Chen, Zaharia and Zou 2023 compared the March and June 2023 versions of GPT-4 and found large behaviour changes, for example accuracy at telling prime from composite numbers dropping from 84% to 51%. A judge running on a moving alias inherits every one of those changes. So does a judge whose prompt someone “just tidied”, whose rubric gained a sentence, or whose sampling temperature changed. Any of these can move scores more than the change being evaluated.

The bar that moves with the season

An interviewer's bar can drift with the pipeline: stricter when there are plenty of strong candidates, looser when a role has been open for months. A score from March and a score from September are then not on the same scale, even with the same interviewer and the same scorecard.

New rating definitions

When the rating definitions are rewritten between cycles, last year's “exceeds” and this year's are different measurements under the same name. That is the prompt-edit problem exactly: the label stayed put while the instrument changed under it.

Palate fatigue

Tasting the fortieth wine of a morning is not the same as tasting the fourth. Competitions break judging into flights and rest the judges partly for this reason, and replicate pours within a flight (Hodgson 2008) are the frozen calibration set of the tasting room.

The end of the shift

Forty radiologists read the same 60 bone examinations before and after a day of clinical reading. Their accuracy (ROC AUC) fell from 0.885 to 0.852, and they reported more fatigue and eye strain (Krupinski et al. 2010). The same reader on the same images is a weaker instrument at the end of the day.

Calls that depend on the last call

Short-range drift, a call that leans against the previous call, is in unit 06. The long-range kind is a zone that moves when the grading system around the umpire changes: in the section below, a pitcher reports umpires who stopped calling the corners once cameras graded them. Same umpire, same rulebook, different instrument.

The fix. Pin the judge to a dated model snapshot, never a “latest” alias. Version the judge prompt, rubric and sampling settings like code, and store the judge version next to every score it produces. Keep a frozen calibration set with human labels and re-run it whenever anything in the judge changes, and on a schedule even when nothing seems to have changed. A drop on the frozen set means the judge moved; only a steady frozen score lets you read a change in the eval as a change in the system. Igor's recipe ends the same way: after launch, keep watching for drift and re-calibrate both humans and judges (step 9). Unit 08 builds the gold set and the audit loop.

When the score becomes the target

The economist Charles Goodhart observed in 1975 that any statistical regularity tends to collapse once pressure is placed on it for control purposes. The anthropologist Marilyn Strathern, writing about exam grades in British universities, gave the version people quote:

When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.— Strathern 1997, crediting Keith Hoskin's statement of Goodhart's law

Every bias on this page is a bias in a judge that nobody is trying to fool. Goodhart's law is what happens when someone is. In LLM work that someone is often an optimiser. Reinforcement learning from human feedback trains a policy to maximise a learned reward model, which is a judge. Gao, Schulman and Hilton 2022 measured what happens as optimisation pushes harder against such a proxy: the true score, from a gold-standard judge, first rises and later falls, even as the proxy's score keeps improving. The same thing happens without any training, in slow motion, whenever a team hill-climbs prompts against an LLM judge. Igor's recipe calls this the hill-climbing funnel and sends only the winners to human review, which is the right defence: the judge picks candidates, humans confirm them.

When the optimiser is a person, or a search over inputs, the weak spots can be startlingly cheap. Raina, Liusie and Gales 2024 found short universal phrases that, appended to any text, pushed LLM judges towards maximum scores, and that the phrases transferred from a surrogate model to unseen judges; absolute scoring was much more vulnerable than pairwise comparison. Zhao et al. 2025 found that a lone colon, a full stop, or an opener like “Thought process:” could earn false positive rewards from LLM judges used as reward models, across many models. And Zheng et al. 2024 showed that a “null model” that returns the same crafted response to every instruction reached an 86.5% length-controlled win rate on AlpacaEval 2.0. None of these outputs is good. They are tuned to the judge.

Teaching to the interview

Once a company's interview format is known, candidates prepare for the format: rehearsed stories shaped to each competency on the scorecard, practice problems in the style the company asks. The scorecard was built to measure ability on the job; under preparation pressure it partly measures preparation. This is teaching to the test with the candidate as their own teacher.

Writing to the template

Employees learn what the rating definitions reward and shape both their work and their self-reviews to it: projects chosen because they will be visible at review time, accomplishments phrased in the template's words. The ratings rise and become less informative about who is doing the most valuable work.

Making wine for the score

Robert Parker's 100-point scores became so influential that critics coined “Parkerization” for winemaking that shifted towards the style he rewarded, with later harvests for riper fruit, lower yields and softer tannins (Robert Parker). Whether that made wine better or merely more alike is still argued. Either way, the producers were optimising against a judge.

Hitting the target

When England's health service was run by published targets with serious consequences for missing them, Gwyn Bevan and Christopher Hood documented reported successes alongside measurement problems and gaming, where performance on the target improved in ways that did not reflect better care (Bevan and Hood 2006). Their title says it: “What's measured is what matters.”

Framing the pitch

Catchers practise framing: catching a borderline pitch with a glove movement that makes it look like a strike to the umpire. The pitch does not change; its appearance to the judge does. That is an adversarial input aimed at a human judge, refined by practice over thousands of pitches, which is what a policy tuned against an LLM judge does at machine speed.

The fix. Keep a human-labelled set the optimiser never touches, and track the gap between judge score and human score as you climb; a widening gap is Goodhart in progress. Give the judge adversarial unit tests: empty answers, the constant “null” response, a bare “Thought process:”, a confident wrong answer, an answer with a note to the grader. A judge that passes any of them is not ready to be optimised against. Prefer pairwise judging where you can, since Raina et al. found it harder to fool. Add every exploit you find to the judge's test set, and cap the pressure: in RLHF, the penalty for straying from the starting model; in prompt hill-climbing, humans confirming every winner.

Watched judges behave differently

The strongest evidence that measuring a judge changes what the judge does comes from baseball. Parsons, Sulaeman, Yates and Hamermesh 2011 studied about 3.5 million pitches from the 2004–2008 seasons. Strikes were called slightly less often when the umpire and pitcher did not share race or ethnicity; on average the effect was small, less than one pitch per game. The interesting part is where it appeared. In 11 of the 30 ballparks, the QuesTec camera system recorded every pitch and graded the umpire's calls, and those grades mattered for umpires' careers. The bias showed up where umpires faced little scrutiny: parks without QuesTec, poorly attended games, and pitches that could not end the at-bat.

With such explicit monitoring, evidence of any race or ethnicity preference vanishes entirely.— Parsons et al. 2011, author's version

Pitchers responded too: when they expected bias, they threw pitches that left the umpire less room for judgement, such as fastballs over the middle of the plate. The judged adapted to the judge.

The paper also records a twist that belongs in the Goodhart section. The pitcher Tom Glavine, who relied on the corners of the zone, said he was told that umpires in his monitored home park would not call strikes on the corners because they did not want the machine to give them poor grades. Grading the judge removed the measurable bias, and it also taught the judge to manage its own grade.

For an LLM judge the lesson splits in two. The model does not know it is being watched, so auditing it will not change its behaviour, only reveal it. The people around it are another matter: whoever writes the judge prompt or optimises against it behaves differently once the judge's agreement with humans is measured and acted on. Run the audit, keep it visible, and keep a slice of it out of reach of whoever is graded by it.

Carry this away. A judge has tastes, a habitual place on the scale, and things it trusts that it should not, and it changes when its model or prompt does. Each of those can be measured on a human-labelled set with a simple check. The one that grows on its own is Goodhart: the harder anything optimises against the judge, the less its score means, so the human check has to stay out of the optimiser's reach.

What you can say now

  • LLM judges can recognise their own outputs and prefer them, and in controlled experiments the preference tracks the recognition (Panickssery et al. 2024); use a judge from a different family, or a mixed panel, and measure the gap by generator.
  • Leniency, severity and central tendency are scale problems, separate from ranking; compare the judge's YES rate and score histogram with the humans' before anything else.
  • Scoring all criteria in one call invites a halo, and credentials or notes to the grader invite deference; score criteria separately, strip metadata, and test the flip rate.
  • LLM judges do not tire, but they change abruptly when the model behind an alias or the prompt changes; pin, version and re-run a frozen set. The hungry-judge parole finding is contested and should not be quoted as settled.
  • Once something is optimised against a judge, Goodhart's law applies: published attacks inflate judge scores with a single token or a constant response, so judges need adversarial tests and a held-out human check.
  • Umpires showed bias mainly where nobody graded their calls; monitoring changes judges, and can also teach them to manage their grade.
