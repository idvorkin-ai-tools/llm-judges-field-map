How judges go wrong II: who is judging

Unit 06 was what the judge is shown. This one is the judge itself: its tastes, where its scale sits, what it defers to, how it changes, and what happens once people optimise against it.
TL;DR — Some errors travel with the judge, not the item: favouring text like its own, a scale set too high, too low or all in the middle, one strong criterion lifting the rest, deference to claimed authority, silent change when the model or prompt changes. Worst, anything optimised to please the judge finds its weak spots, and the score stops meaning quality. The human fixes carry over: a judge from another family, an anchored scale, one criterion per call, hidden authorship, a pinned judge, and a human check the optimiser never sees.

A judge is not a neutral pipe from output to score. Personnel psychologists spent the twentieth century naming its tastes and blind spots in rating forms. A model has its own versions, plus a problem people rarely have: an optimiser that can try millions of variations to find what the judge likes.

Throughout, the judge is an interviewera managera competition judgea radiologista plate umpire, and the YES call is “hire”“exceeds expectations”“medal”“recall”“strike”.

Favouring its own kind

Self-preference is a judge scoring its own model's outputs higher than humans would. The MT-Bench paper saw GPT-4 give itself about a 10% higher win rate than humans did, and Claude-v1 about 25%, but said its data could not settle whether this was a real bias (Zheng et al. 2023). Panickssery, Bowman and Feng 2024 controlled it on news summaries: GPT-3.5, GPT-4 and Llama 2 all favoured their own over other models' and humans'. All three recognised their own text better than chance, GPT-4 73.5% of the time, and fine-tuning that recognition up or down moved self-preference with it, roughly linearly. The judge appears to prefer its output because it recognises it.

The wider version is familiarity: a same-family judge, the common setup, likes that family's phrasing and hedges.

Hiring people like me

Lauren Rivera interviewed 120 people who hired for elite professional-services firms and sat in on a hiring committee. Evaluators sought candidates who shared their leisure pursuits, experiences and self-presentation, and that similarity often outweighed concerns about productivity (Rivera 2012). “Reminds me of me” is a model rewarding text like its own.

Rating your own hires

A manager who argued to hire someone now rates that person's work, and every rating is also a verdict on the manager's judgement. The fix is the one this page recommends for models: someone without that stake reviews the rating, and calibration compares it with people the manager did not hire.

A palate trained on one style

Blind pouring stops a judge scoring their own wine. The subtle version survives: a winemaker who judges recognises the style they make, the oak or the ripeness, and wines that taste like home taste right. The blind glass hides the label, not the style, exactly the gap a model's self-recognition uses.

Reading your own earlier report

A radiologist comparing today's image with a prior they reported is checking their own work. In double reading the second reader's value is independence; one who sees the first call is closer to a rubber stamp. Independent reads are medicine's way of not letting a judge grade its own output.

Calling for your own side

The best-measured case is behind the plate: umpires called strikes more often when they shared the pitcher's race or ethnicity, mainly where nobody was checking (Parsons et al. 2011). That last part gets its own section below; it is the most useful finding on this page.

The defence is centuries old: China's imperial examinations had clerks recopy every script so examiners could not recognise handwriting (unit 01). The LLM version: a judge from a different family, or a mixed panel, with surface style normalised as Panickssery et al. did. To measure it, split judge-minus-human scores by which model wrote the output; the gap on the judge's own family is your self-preference.

Where the judge sits on the scale

Rating research has three names for a scale in the wrong place. Leniency: scores run high. Severity: scores run low. Central tendency: nearly everything gets the middle score. All three can coexist with good ranking: a lenient judge may order items correctly and still pass far too many. That is why unit 05 separates the ranking (the ROC curve, AUC 0.868 in the casebook) from the cut: leniency is an effective cut lower than the policy's.

Try thisMove the pass cut. The lenient judge matches the reference's 40 passes at a cut of 5, the severe one nearly does at 3; the compressed judge jumps from 88 to 12 and no cut gets close.

LLM judges show all three. Central tendency is best documented: the G-Eval authors saw one digit, such as 3, dominate an LLM's 1–5 scores, shrinking the variance and the correlation with humans (Liu et al. 2023). Their fix weights each score by the probability of its token instead of taking the printed digit. Practitioners also see judges that give nearly everything a 4. A scale used on one or two points carries little more information than a coin.

The casebook has a mild case. The easy interviewer and the hard one

Every panel knows the interviewer who passes almost everyone and the one who rejects almost everyone. Both still rank, but the outcome depends on who the candidate drew, and votes counted without each interviewer's base rate are numbers in different units.

The generous manager

A manager who rates most of the team “exceeds” and one who rates almost nobody that way can be describing equally strong teams. Calibration meetings exist mainly to translate one manager's “exceeds” into another's.

The high scorer at the table

On a panel, one judge's 90 is another's 86. Medals by panel average depend on who sat on the panel, one reason some panels reconcile scores by discussion rather than averaging.

Ten radiologists, ten thresholds

Ten radiologists read the same 150 mammograms; the share of cancer-free women they would have sent for immediate work-up ranged from 11% to 65%, and for women with cancer from 74% to 96% (Elmore et al. 1994). Same films, very different thresholds, measured against biopsy and follow-up.

The pitcher's umpire and the hitter's umpire

Players talk about big-zone and tight-zone umpires. Pitch tracking turns the gossip into a number: each umpire's called-strike rate on pitches in the same locations. A big-zone umpire is a lenient judge, with a cut below the rulebook's.

The fix. Compare the judge's YES rate (or mean score) with the humans' on a labelled set. Anchor the scale with examples of a 2 and a 4 (unit 02). Or drop it: Hamel Husain advises pass/fail with a written critique, since nobody can say what separates a 3 from a 4 (Husain). A binary judge's leniency is one number you can read off a confusion matrix and move by editing the prompt.

One good trait lifting the rest

In 1920 Edward Thorndike found that officers' ratings of their men on separate qualities, such as physique, intellect and leadership, correlated far more than the qualities plausibly could: raters formed one general impression and let it colour every line (Thorndike 1920). That is the halo effect; its negative twin, one bad trait dragging the rest down, is sometimes called the horns effect.

An LLM asked to “rate faithfulness, completeness, clarity and tone, 1–5 each” in one call writes four numbers, each conditioned on the ones before, so they will likely move together. This page knows of no study sizing the LLM halo; treat it as a risk to measure. If the judge's criterion scores correlate at 0.9 where the humans' correlate at 0.4, the rubric has collapsed into one score. Sharper: insert a factual error into a fluent answer and see whether tone and clarity fall too.

Scoring competencies one at a time

Structured interviews give each interviewer specific competencies, a score per question against anchored answers, and no comparing notes until everyone has scored. Each rule is a halo defence: fewer criteria per judge, evidence before the number, no contamination from the room.

The project that rated everything

One visible launch can lift every line of a review, including collaboration, which the launch says nothing about. Asking for a specific example under each dimension exposes the halo: a dimension with no example of its own is borrowing its score.

The twenty-point sheet

Splitting a wine into appearance, nose, palate and finish helps only if each part is scored on its own evidence. A judge who has decided “this is a gold” fills in the parts to add up to it.

The report form

A BI-RADS report records breast density and individual findings separately from the final assessment category (BI-RADS), so the call follows from the findings rather than the findings being filled in to match the call.

Status as a halo

With pitch-tracking data, Jerry Kim and Brayden King found umpires more likely to call borderline pitches strikes for high-status pitchers, and less likely to miss their true strikes, errors that changed those pitchers' results (Kim and King 2014). Reputation, a fact about the person, lifted the score of the pitch.

The fix. Score each criterion in its own call, with only its definition in the prompt; Igor's evals recipe defines a rubric as a per-task checklist whose items are checked separately (step 4). If cost forces one call, require evidence under each criterion before any number, and the overall score last. Prefer narrow binary checks (“does the answer cite the clause it relies on?”), which leave less room for an impression to leak in.

Deferring to claimed authority

A judge with authority bias scores who the content claims to come from. Chen et al. 2024 found human and LLM judges both swayed by fake references; as an attack, fake references and rich formatting succeeded up to 50% of the time on GPT-4. The CALM framework counts authority among twelve LLM-judge biases (Ye et al. 2024). It covers “As a board-certified physician…”, unchecked citations, and text in the output telling the judge what score to give.

Models are also trained to agree with the person they talk to, so a prompt mentioning “the user believes this is correct”, or an earlier verdict, invites sycophancy. Anything in the context that is not the output, the policy or the reference is a lever.

The senior referral

A candidate referred by a senior leader, or with a famous employer on the CV, walks in with a borrowed score. Some processes hide referral source and pedigree from skills interviewers, as the judge prompt should hide the generator's name.

The senior voice in calibration

In calibration, the most senior person's view of an employee tends to become the room's. Having each manager state a rating before discussion, with evidence rather than impressions, guards against it.

The price on the label

People told a wine cost more rated it more pleasant, with more activity in a brain region linked to experienced pleasantness (Plassmann et al. 2008). They were volunteers, not competition judges; competitions pour blind so nobody has to find out how far a famous label moves a professional.

The note on the request

“Senior colleague thinks this is benign” on a request form is not evidence about the image. Clinical history can be legitimate; an opinion about the answer is not. Independent double reading keeps the second read from being the first with a second signature.

The star pitcher

The status finding above is also an authority finding: a well-known pitcher gets the edges on reputation. A catcher or manager arguing a call applies the same pressure live.

The fix. Strip author identity, model names, claimed credentials and prior verdicts. Fence the output off as data that addresses nothing to the judge. Verify citations with a tool. And test: add a fake credential or reference to graded outputs and count the flips. The flip rate is your authority bias.

The judge changes over time

Human judges drift with fatigue and time of day. The famous claim is the “hungry judge”: Israeli parole boards granted about 65% of requests at the start of each session, falling to nearly zero before a food break and jumping back after it (Danziger, Levav and Avnaim-Pesso 2011). Treat it as contested. A letter in the same journal reported that case order was not random: boards finished one prison's cases before a break, and prisoners without lawyers, who succeeded far less often, usually went last (Weinshall-Margel and Shapard 2011). Later simulations argued that scheduling could produce at least part of the pattern and that the effect is overestimated (Glöckner 2016). Firmer evidence of fatigue comes from radiology, below.

An LLM judge does not tire. It drifts more abruptly: the provider updates the model behind a name. Chen, Zaharia and Zou 2023 found large changes between the March and June 2023 versions of GPT-4; accuracy at telling prime from composite numbers fell from 84% to 51%. A judge on a moving alias inherits that, as does one whose prompt someone “just tidied”; either can move scores more than the change being evaluated.

The bar that moves with the season

An interviewer's bar drifts with the pipeline: stricter when strong candidates are plentiful, looser when a role has been open for months. March and September scores are then on different scales, same interviewer, same scorecard.

New rating definitions

Rewrite the rating definitions between cycles and last year's “exceeds” and this year's are different measurements under one name: the prompt-edit problem exactly.

Palate fatigue

The fortieth wine of a morning is not tasted like the fourth. Competitions break judging into flights and rest the judges, and replicate pours within a flight (Hodgson 2008) are the tasting room's frozen calibration set.

The end of the shift

Forty radiologists read the same 60 bone examinations before and after a day of clinical reading. Their accuracy (ROC AUC) fell from 0.885 to 0.852, and they reported more fatigue and eye strain (Krupinski et al. 2010).

Calls that depend on the last call

Short-range drift, a call leaning against the previous one, is in unit 06. The long-range kind is a zone that moves when the grading around the umpire changes: below, a pitcher reports umpires who stopped calling the corners once cameras graded them.

The fix. Pin the judge to a dated model snapshot, never a “latest” alias. Version prompt, rubric and sampling settings like code, and log the judge version with every score. Re-run a frozen, human-labelled calibration set on every judge change and on a schedule; a drop means the judge moved, not the system. Igor's recipe ends the same way: keep watching for drift (step 9). Unit 08 builds the gold set and the audit loop.

When the score becomes the target

The economist Charles Goodhart observed in 1975 that any statistical regularity tends to collapse once pressure is placed on it for control purposes. Marilyn Strathern, writing about British university grades, gave the quoted version:

When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.— Strathern 1997, crediting Keith Hoskin's statement of Goodhart's law

Every bias above is in a judge nobody is trying to fool. Goodhart's law is what happens when someone is, and in LLM work that someone is often an optimiser. Reinforcement learning from human feedback trains a policy to maximise a learned reward model, which is a judge. Gao, Schulman and Hilton 2022 found that as optimisation pushes harder against such a proxy, the true score from a gold-standard judge rises and then falls while the proxy's keeps improving. Hill-climbing prompts against an LLM judge does the same in slow motion; Igor's hill-climbing funnel sends only the winners to human review.

Try thisPush the optimisation pressure up and watch the judge's score climb while the true score peaks and falls. A weaker judge (bigger blind spot) peaks sooner and lower.

When the optimiser is a person or a search over inputs, the weak spots can be startlingly cheap. Raina, Liusie and Gales 2024 found short universal phrases that push LLM judges towards maximum scores and transfer from a surrogate model to unseen judges; absolute scoring was much more vulnerable than pairwise. Zhao et al. 2025 found that a lone colon, a full stop, or “Thought process:” earned false rewards from LLM judges used as reward models. Zheng et al. 2024 showed a “null model” returning the same crafted response to every instruction reached an 86.5% length-controlled win rate on AlpacaEval 2.0. None of these outputs is good; they are tuned to the judge.

Teaching to the interview

Once a company's interview format is known, candidates prepare for it: rehearsed stories shaped to each competency, practice problems in the house style. The scorecard then partly measures preparation. This is teaching to the test, with the candidate as their own teacher.

Writing to the template

Employees learn what the rating definitions reward and shape their work and self-reviews to it: projects picked for visibility, accomplishments in the template's words. Ratings rise and say less about who does the most valuable work.

Making wine for the score

Robert Parker's 100-point scores became so influential that critics coined “Parkerization” for winemaking that shifted towards the style he rewarded: later harvests for riper fruit, lower yields, softer tannins (Robert Parker). Better wine or merely more alike is still argued; either way, producers optimised against a judge.

Hitting the target

When England's health service was run by published targets with serious consequences, Gwyn Bevan and Christopher Hood documented successes alongside measurement problems and gaming: target performance that did not reflect better care (Bevan and Hood 2006). Their title: “What's measured is what matters.”

Framing the pitch

Catchers practise framing: a glove movement that makes a borderline pitch look like a strike. The pitch does not change; its appearance to the judge does. An adversarial input refined over thousands of pitches, which a policy tuned against an LLM judge does at machine speed.

The fix. Keep a human-labelled set the optimiser never touches, and track the gap between judge and human scores as you climb; a widening gap is Goodhart in progress. Give the judge adversarial unit tests (an empty answer, the “null” response, a bare “Thought process:”, a confident wrong answer, a note to the grader); a judge that passes any of them is not ready to be optimised against. Prefer pairwise judging, which Raina et al. found harder to fool. Add every exploit to the test set, and cap the pressure: in RLHF, the penalty for straying from the starting model; in prompt hill-climbing, humans confirming every winner.

Watched judges behave differently

The strongest evidence that measuring a judge changes the judge comes from baseball. Parsons, Sulaeman, Yates and Hamermesh 2011 studied about 3.5 million pitches from 2004–2008. Strikes were called slightly less often when umpire and pitcher did not share race or ethnicity, under one pitch per game on average. The interesting part is where. In 11 of 30 ballparks QuesTec cameras graded every call, and the grades mattered for careers. The bias showed up where scrutiny was low: parks without QuesTec, poorly attended games, and pitches that could not end the at-bat.

With such explicit monitoring, evidence of any race or ethnicity preference vanishes entirely.— Parsons et al. 2011, author's version

Pitchers adapted too, throwing pitches that left less room for judgement, such as fastballs over the middle.

The paper also records a Goodhart twist. Tom Glavine, a pitcher who relied on the corners, said he was told umpires in his monitored home park would not call strikes on the corners because they did not want poor grades from the machine. Grading the judge removed the bias and taught it to manage its grade.

An LLM judge does not know it is watched, so an audit only reveals it. The people around it do: whoever writes the prompt or optimises against it behaves differently once the judge's agreement with humans is measured. Run the audit, keep it visible, and keep a slice out of reach of whoever it grades.

Carry this away. A judge has tastes, a place on the scale and misplaced trust, and it changes when its model or prompt does. Each can be measured on a human-labelled set with a simple check. Goodhart grows on its own: the harder anything optimises against the judge, the less its score means, so keep the human check out of the optimiser's reach.

What you can say now

  • LLM judges recognise and prefer their own outputs, and the preference tracks the recognition (Panickssery et al. 2024); use a different-family judge or mixed panel and measure the gap by generator.
  • Leniency, severity and central tendency are scale problems, separate from ranking; compare YES rates and score histograms with the humans' first.
  • One call for all criteria invites a halo; credentials and notes to the grader invite deference. Score criteria separately, strip metadata, test the flip rate.
  • LLM judges change abruptly when the model or prompt does: pin, version, re-run a frozen set. The hungry-judge parole finding is contested; don't quote it as settled.
  • Optimising against a judge invokes Goodhart's law: a single token or a constant response can inflate scores, so judges need adversarial tests and a held-out human check.
  • Umpires showed bias mainly where nobody graded their calls; monitoring changes judges, and can teach them to manage their grade.
