Appendix: the maths, worked

Every number on the site, derived from its definition and worked by hand on the shared casebook. The arithmetic is identical in every domain; only the nouns change.
TL;DR — Judging statistics come in three families. Agreement statistics (percent agreement, Cohen's kappa, Scott's pi, Fleiss' kappa, Krippendorff's alpha, weighted kappa) compare judges with each other and subtract the agreement that chance alone would produce. Validity statistics (sensitivity, specificity, precision, Youden's J, ROC, AUC) compare a judge with whatever stands in for the truth. Uncertainty statistics (Wilson intervals, kappa standard errors, the bootstrap) say how much any of those numbers would move with a different sample of items. Every one of them is a few lines of arithmetic on a small table, and this page does that arithmetic in full, so nothing on the other pages has to be taken on trust.

Each section below states a formula, says where it comes from, and then works it on the casebook numbers the rest of the site uses. All of the numbers were computed by script and re-derived by hand here; where a number is rounded, the unrounded value is shown first. Formulas are written in plain notation: n is the number of items, p is a proportion, and a 2×2 table has cells a, b, c, d read left to right, top to bottom.

The casebook has three groups of numbers. A two-judge table of 100 items for agreement, rated by interviewers Ana and Benmanagers Carla and Devjudges Elodie and FemiDr Gale and Dr Hart, double readingumpires Ivo and June, calling the same pitches on video; a three-judge version of 100 items; and a judge-versus-truth table of 200 items, 60 of them truly YES, where the truth is how the hire actually did a year later (which only exists for people who were hired, so read this as an idealised study)a calibration committee's consensus rating, since reviews have no outside trutha reference panel's verdict, since wine quality has no outside truthbiopsy or a year of clean follow-upthe pitch-tracking camera and the rulebook zone. The YES label is “hire”“exceeds expectations”“medal”“recall the patient”“strike”; for an LLM judge it is “passes the policy”.

Agreement beyond chance: percent agreement, Cohen's kappa, Scott's pi

Start with the two-judge table. Each of 100 items is judged by both judges, and the table counts the four possible pairs of verdicts.

Here judge A is AnaCarlaElodieDr GaleIvo and judge B is BenDevFemiDr HartJune. For an LLM judge, A and B are two human labelers, or one labeler and the model.

Percent agreement is the share of items on the diagonal:

po = (a + d) / n
Worked

po = (45 + 30) / 100 = 0.75.

The trouble with 0.75 is that two judges who never looked at the items would still agree some of the time. If A says YES 60% of the time and B says YES 55% of the time, and they decide independently, they both say YES on 0.60 × 0.55 of items and both say NO on 0.40 × 0.45. That sum is the chance agreement:

pe = pA,yes · pB,yes + pA,no · pB,no
Worked

pA,yes = 60/100 = 0.60, pB,yes = 55/100 = 0.55.

pe = 0.60 × 0.55 + 0.40 × 0.45 = 0.33 + 0.18 = 0.51.

Cohen's kappa (Cohen 1960) asks how much of the room above chance the judges actually used. The numerator is agreement beyond chance; the denominator is the most agreement beyond chance that was possible.

κ = (po − pe) / (1 − pe)
Worked

κ = (0.75 − 0.51) / (1 − 0.51) = 0.24 / 0.49 = 0.4898 ≈ 0.49.

Reading: the judges closed about half of the gap between chance agreement and perfect agreement. On the widely quoted (and admittedly arbitrary) Landis and Koch bands, 0.41–0.60 is “moderate” (Landis and Koch 1977).

Kappa is 1 for perfect agreement, 0 when the judges agree exactly as often as chance predicts, and negative when they agree less often than that. It is not a percentage of anything, and a kappa of 0.49 does not mean “they agree 49% of the time”.

Scott's pi (Scott 1955) is the older sibling. It uses the same formula but computes chance agreement from the two judges' pooled YES rate, as if both judges drew from one shared distribution. Kappa lets each judge keep their own rate.

p̄ = (pA,yes + pB,yes) / 2    pe,π = p̄² + (1 − p̄)²    π = (po − pe,π) / (1 − pe,π)
Worked

p̄ = (0.60 + 0.55) / 2 = 0.575. pe,π = 0.575² + 0.425² = 0.330625 + 0.180625 = 0.51125.

π = (0.75 − 0.51125) / (1 − 0.51125) = 0.23875 / 0.48875 = 0.4885 ≈ 0.489.

Pi is never larger than kappa, because pooled rates can only make chance agreement look bigger. The two differ noticeably only when the judges' YES rates differ a lot; here 60% against 55% moves the third decimal. Which one to report is a question about what “chance” means: if the judges really have different thresholds, kappa credits that difference as part of the judges; pi treats it as disagreement.

What the 0.49 means at a hiring panel

Ana says hire on 60 of 100 candidates and Ben on 55. Two interviewers flipping coins weighted that way would agree on 51 candidates; Ana and Ben agree on 75. They captured 24 of the 49 agreements that chance left on the table. That is a usable panel, but far from one interviewer standing in for the other.

What the 0.49 means in calibration

Carla rates 60 of 100 employees “exceeds” and Dev 55. Rating at those rates without reading anyone's work would match on 51 people; they match on 75. Half of the possible agreement above chance is there, which is why a calibration meeting still has plenty to argue about.

What the 0.49 means at the tasting table

Elodie medals 60 of 100 wines and Femi 55. Two judges awarding medals at those rates at random would agree on 51 wines; they agree on 75. The panel is tasting something real, but swapping one judge for the other would change roughly one verdict in four.

What the 0.49 means in double reading

Dr Gale recalls 60 of 100 patients and Dr Hart 55 (an enriched test set, not a screening population). Independent readers at those rates would agree on 51 images; they agree on 75. That is the gap double reading exists to exploit: each reader catches things the other misses.

What the 0.49 means behind the plate

Ivo calls 60 of 100 taken pitches strikes and June 55 (a set chosen to be borderline). Umpires calling at those rates blindly would agree on 51 pitches; they agree on 75. Moderate agreement is what borderline pitches produce, and it is exactly why the camera exists.

The kappa paradoxes: high agreement, low kappa

Now make YES rare. Two judges look at 100 items; they both say NO on 90, both say YES on 2, and split 4 and 4 on the rest.

Worked

po = (2 + 90) / 100 = 0.92.

Each judge says YES on 6%. pe = 0.06 × 0.06 + 0.94 × 0.94 = 0.0036 + 0.8836 = 0.8872.

κ = (0.92 − 0.8872) / (1 − 0.8872) = 0.0328 / 0.1128 = 0.2908 ≈ 0.29.

Ninety-two percent agreement and a kappa that looks poor. Nothing is broken: when nearly everything is NO, two judges who both say NO almost always will agree by default, so chance agreement is already 0.887 and there is only 0.113 of room left to earn. Of the 10 items where at least one judge said YES, the judges agreed on only 2. Kappa is reporting that, correctly.

Feinstein and Cicchetti named two paradoxes (Feinstein and Cicchetti 1990). In the first, the one above, a high po is pulled sharply down by unbalanced marginal totals. In the second, kappa comes out higher when the two judges' marginals are unbalanced in different directions than when they are balanced the same way. You can see the second one on the casebook: keep po = 0.75 but move all 25 disagreements into one cell (A YES, B NO), so the judges' YES rates become 70% and 45%. Chance agreement drops to 0.70 × 0.45 + 0.30 × 0.55 = 0.48, and kappa rises to (0.75 − 0.48) / 0.52 = 0.519, above the casebook's 0.49, although the judges now disagree in a more systematic way.

Byrt, Bishop and Carlin turned this into bookkeeping (Byrt et al. 1993). They define a prevalence index and a bias index from the 2×2 table, plus a prevalence- and bias-adjusted kappa (PABAK), and show that kappa is a function of the three:

PI = (a − d) / n    BI = (b − c) / n    PABAK = 2po − 1    κ = (PABAK − PI² + BI²) / (1 − PI² + BI²)

The identity makes both paradoxes mechanical. A large prevalence index (lots of one kind of agreement) is squared and subtracted, so it drags kappa down. A bias index is squared and added, so judges who disagree in one direction get a kappa bump. Cicchetti and Feinstein's companion paper recommends always reporting two more numbers next to kappa: agreement on the positives and agreement on the negatives (Cicchetti and Feinstein 1990).

ppos = 2a / (2a + b + c)    pneg = 2d / (2d + b + c)
Worked

Rare YES: ppos = 4 / 12 = 0.333; pneg = 180 / 188 = 0.957. The judges agree on NO and barely agree on YES. That one sentence explains the 0.29 better than the 0.29 does.

Casebook two judges: ppos = 90 / 115 = 0.783; pneg = 60 / 85 = 0.706.

Rare YES in hiring

A senior role where interviewers say hire on 6 of 100 candidates. Ana and Ben agree on 92 of 100 verdicts, which sounds like a calibrated pair, but of the 10 people either of them wanted, they agreed on 2. The 92% is mostly two people agreeing that most candidates are not a fit.

Rare YES in reviews

An organisation where “exceeds” goes to about 6% of employees. Carla and Dev match on 92 of 100 ratings, but they agree on only 2 of the 10 people either one put forward. The quota makes them look calibrated when they disagree about almost everyone who matters for the top rating.

Rare YES in wine

A strict competition medals about 6% of entries. Elodie and Femi agree on 92 of 100 glasses, almost all of them “no medal”. On the 10 wines either judge wanted to medal, they agreed twice. The medals are the part people read, and that is where the panel is weakest.

Rare YES in screening

Screening is the textbook case: most images are normal. Dr Gale and Dr Hart agree on 92 of 100 reads, nearly all “clear”, but of the 10 patients either wanted recalled they agreed on 2. Percent agreement in a low-prevalence population says little about the cases that matter.

Rare YES behind the plate

Take a set of pitches well outside the zone where strikes are rare. Ivo and June agree on 92 of 100 calls, mostly obvious balls, but of the 10 pitches either called a strike they agreed on 2. The agreement number is inflated by pitches nobody would miss.

For LLM judges this is the common case, not an edge case. A safety or hallucination judge looks for a failure that appears in a few percent of outputs, so “the judge agrees with humans 95% of the time” can sit next to a kappa of 0.3. Report ppos and pneg, or move straight to the confusion matrix below. See also Igor's notes on agreement, kappa and friends.

Fleiss' kappa: three or more judges

With three judges per item there is no single 2×2 table. Fleiss' kappa (Fleiss 1971) works item by item instead: on each item, what fraction of the pairs of judges agree? Then it compares the average with the same fraction expected by chance from the pooled category rates. Because chance uses pooled rates, it generalises Scott's pi, not Cohen's kappa.

The casebook has 100 items, three judges each, summarised by how many YES votes an item received:

Per item with m judges and nij votes for category j, Fleiss' agreement is the share of the m(m − 1) ordered pairs that match:

Pi = Σj nij(nij − 1) / (m(m − 1))    P̄ = mean of Pi    pj = share of all votes in category j    Pe = Σj pj²    κF = (P̄ − Pe) / (1 − Pe)
Worked

Unanimous item: (3·2 + 0) / 6 = 1. Split item (2 YES, 1 NO): (2·1 + 1·0) / 6 = 1/3.

P̄ = (73 × 1 + 27 × 1/3) / 100 = (73 + 9) / 100 = 0.82. That is the mean pairwise agreement: 246 of the 300 judge pairs agree.

pyes = 147 / 300 = 0.49. Pe = 0.49² + 0.51² = 0.2401 + 0.2601 = 0.5002.

κF = (0.82 − 0.5002) / (1 − 0.5002) = 0.3198 / 0.4998 = 0.6399 ≈ 0.64.

Note the shape of the data: 73 of 100 items are unanimous, and the 27 split items are split 2–1, because with three judges and two labels no other split exists. A single number hides which items those are; in practice the split items are the useful output, because they are the ones worth a human read.

Three interviewers

A loop of three interviewers per candidate: 35 unanimous hires, 38 unanimous no-hires, 27 split decisions. The 27 are the candidates the debrief meeting should spend its time on, and the 0.64 says the loop is consistent enough that unanimity means something.

Three managers

Three managers rate each employee in a calibration exercise: 73 unanimous, 27 split. A calibration meeting that spends equal time on all 100 people is wasting most of it; the split 27 are where the rating definitions are not doing their job.

A panel of three

Three judges at one table taste each wine: 35 unanimous medals, 38 unanimous no-medals, 27 split. Many competitions resolve splits by discussion or a fourth taster, which is exactly where the 27 should go.

Three readers

Three radiologists read each image in a test set: 73 unanimous, 27 split. Arbitration in double-reading programmes exists for the split cases; the kappa of 0.64 says the readers are consistent enough that the unanimous ones can mostly be trusted.

Three umpires on video

Three umpires call the same 100 pitches from video: 73 unanimous, 27 split 2–1. The splits are the pitches near the edge of the zone, where the camera has the final word anyway.

Krippendorff's alpha: the coincidence matrix

Krippendorff's alpha (Hayes and Krippendorff 2007) is written in terms of disagreement rather than agreement, and it handles any number of judges, missing ratings and ordered or numeric scales in one formula:

α = 1 − Do / De    (observed disagreement over expected disagreement)

The device that makes it easy is the coincidence matrix. Forget which judge said what. For each item, take every ordered pair of values given to it by different judges, and add each pair to a values-by-values table, weighting each pair by 1/(mu − 1), where mu is the number of judges who rated that item. Every rating then contributes exactly one unit to the table, so the table sums to the total number of ratings, n. The matrix is symmetric, its diagonal holds the matching pairs, and its row totals nv are how often each value was used. With that matrix, for any distance function δ:

α = 1 − (n − 1) · Σ ovv' δ(v, v') / Σ nv nv' δ(v, v')

This is the form given in the Krippendorff's alpha article's worked example; the (n − 1) is the small-sample correction that makes expected disagreement come from drawing two values without replacement.

Worked · two judges

Each item has m = 2 ratings, so each item contributes its two ordered pairs with weight 1/(2 − 1) = 1. The 45 both-YES items give 90 YES-YES entries; the 30 both-NO items give 60 NO-NO; the 15 + 10 = 25 split items give 25 YES-NO and 25 NO-YES. Totals: nyes = 90 + 25 = 115, nno = 60 + 25 = 85, n = 200.

Nominal distance: δ = 1 for different values, 0 for the same. Σ o δ = 25 + 25 = 50. Σ nvnv'δ = 115·85 + 85·115 = 19,550.

α = 1 − 199 × 50 / 19,550 = 1 − 9,950 / 19,550 = 1 − 0.5090 = 0.4910 ≈ 0.491.

Equivalently Do = 50/200 = 0.25 and De = 19,550 / (200 × 199) = 0.4912, so α = 1 − 0.25/0.4912.

Worked · three judges

Now m = 3, so each item has 3 × 2 = 6 ordered pairs, each weighted 1/2. A unanimous YES item puts 6 × ½ = 3 into YES-YES; 35 of them give 105. A 2–1 YES item has 2 YES-YES pairs (1 entry), and 4 mixed pairs (2 entries, split 1 YES-NO and 1 NO-YES); 15 of them give 15 YES-YES and 15 in each mixed cell. A 1–2 item gives 1 NO-NO entry and 1 in each mixed cell; 12 of them give 12 NO-NO and 12 per mixed cell. Unanimous NO: 38 × 3 = 114 NO-NO.

oyes,yes = 105 + 15 = 120; ono,no = 114 + 12 = 126; oyes,no = ono,yes = 15 + 12 = 27. nyes = 147, nno = 153, n = 300.

α = 1 − 299 × 54 / (2 × 147 × 153) = 1 − 16,146 / 44,982 = 1 − 0.3589 = 0.6411 ≈ 0.641.

Alpha lands just above Scott's pi and Fleiss' kappa (0.491 against 0.489; 0.641 against 0.640). That is the (n − 1) correction at work: with 200 or 300 ratings it barely matters, and on 20 ratings it would. All three use pooled value frequencies for chance, so on complete binary data they are near-identical, and alpha's advantages show up elsewhere: missing ratings (a judge who skipped an item simply contributes fewer pairs) and scales with order.

For ordered or numeric scales, only δ changes. Nominal: δ = 1 if the values differ. Interval: δ(v, v') = (v − v')², so being two points off costs four times as much as one. Ordinal uses a rank-based distance that depends on how many ratings fall between the two values, so it respects order without assuming the steps are equal. The weighted-kappa example in the next section shows the effect: on that 3×3 table, nominal alpha is 0.535 and interval alpha is 0.657 (computed in the scratch script from the coincidence matrix of the table), close to unweighted and quadratic-weighted kappa respectively.

Weighted kappa: partial credit for near misses

Plain kappa treats every disagreement as equally bad. On a scale with order, that is wrong: one judge saying “partial” and the other “pass” is a smaller disagreement than “fail” against “pass”. Cohen's weighted kappa (Cohen 1968) gives each cell a weight and computes agreement with those weights. In agreement-weight form, with k categories numbered 1 to k:

linear: wij = 1 − |i − j| / (k − 1)    quadratic: wij = 1 − (i − j)² / (k − 1)²
po,w = Σ wij pij    pe,w = Σ wij pi· p·j    κw = (po,w − pe,w) / (1 − pe,w)

Setting every off-diagonal weight to 0 gives back plain kappa. Quadratic weights have a useful property: Fleiss and Cohen showed that quadratic-weighted kappa is equivalent to an intraclass correlation coefficient, so it behaves like a correlation on numeric scores (Fleiss and Cohen 1973).

Kappas. Unweighted: (0.69 − 0.3366) / 0.6634 = 0.533. Linear: (0.825 − 0.5712) / 0.4288 = 0.592. Quadratic: (0.8925 − 0.6885) / 0.3115 = 0.655.

The same table gives three kappas, from 0.53 to 0.65, and none of them is wrong. Most disagreements here are one step apart, so the more partial credit a scheme gives for one-step misses, the higher it scores. That makes the weighting a policy choice to state up front, not a knob to turn after seeing results. A quadratic-weighted kappa is not comparable with an unweighted one from another study.

The confusion matrix: judge against truth

Agreement statistics compare judges with each other. Validity statistics compare a judge with the truth, and the base object is the confusion matrix. The casebook: 200 items, 60 truly YES.

Youden's J: one number for a yes/no judge

Youden proposed a single index for a diagnostic test (Youden 1950):

J = sensitivity + specificity − 1 = TPR − FPR
Worked

J = 0.80 + 0.85 − 1 = 0.65. Equivalently TPR − FPR = 0.80 − 0.15 = 0.65, since FPR = 1 − specificity = 21/140 = 0.15.

Always-YES judge: 1 + 0 − 1 = 0. Always-NO judge: 0 + 1 − 1 = 0. A coin flip at any bias: TPR = FPR, so J = 0.

The second form is the useful one. J is how much more often the judge says YES to truly-YES items than to truly-NO items. Any judge whose verdict ignores the item, however it is biased, says YES at the same rate to both, and scores 0. A perfect judge scores 1, and a judge below 0 is informative but inverted: flip its labels. Unlike accuracy, J does not reward matching the majority class, and unlike precision it does not move with prevalence, because both of its parts are row ratios.

Geometry. Plot the judge as a point (FPR, TPR) in ROC space. The diagonal TPR = FPR is every judge that ignores the input. J is the vertical height of the judge's point above that diagonal: here, the point (0.15, 0.80) sits 0.80 − 0.15 = 0.65 above the diagonal at x = 0.15. When a judge has a threshold to choose, the cut that maximises J is the point on the ROC curve farthest above the diagonal; the Youden's J article describes it as the height above the chance line and notes Peirce published the same formula in 1884.

Balanced accuracy is the mean of sensitivity and specificity, and it is J in different units:

balanced accuracy = (sensitivity + specificity) / 2 = (J + 1) / 2
Worked

(0.80 + 0.85) / 2 = 0.825 = (0.65 + 1) / 2. Chance is 0.5 on this scale and 0 on J's.

J weights a missed YES and a false YES equally per item of each class. If the costs are unequal, and in most domains they are, the best operating point is not J's maximum; it is wherever the cost-weighted trade-off is best. J is a summary, not a decision rule.

Base rates: precision from sensitivity, specificity and prevalence

Precision is the question people actually ask of a verdict: “the judge said YES; how likely is it to be right?” Bayes' rule gives it from the judge's two row rates and the prevalence π of truly-YES items:

precision = sens·π / (sens·π + (1 − spec)·(1 − π))    NPV = spec·(1 − π) / (spec·(1 − π) + (1 − sens)·π)
Worked · casebook, π = 30%

Numerator 0.80 × 0.30 = 0.24. False-YES mass 0.15 × 0.70 = 0.105.

precision = 0.24 / (0.24 + 0.105) = 0.24 / 0.345 = 0.696, matching 48/69 from the table. NPV = 0.595 / (0.595 + 0.06) = 0.908.

Worked · same judge, π = 5%, 1,000 items

50 truly YES, 950 truly NO. TP = 0.80 × 50 = 40. FP = 0.15 × 950 = 142.5.

precision = 40 / (40 + 142.5) = 40 / 182.5 = 0.219. NPV = 807.5 / (807.5 + 10) = 0.988.

For comparison (same formula): at π = 1%, precision = 0.008 / (0.008 + 0.1485) = 0.051; at π = 50%, 0.40 / 0.475 = 0.842.

The judge did not change. Its sensitivity and specificity are the same in both rows; only the mix of items changed, and precision fell from 0.70 to 0.22. At 5% prevalence, nearly four of every five YES verdicts are wrong. This is the reason a judge validated on a balanced test set can disappoint on production traffic, where the failure it hunts for is rare.

Base rates in hiring

An interviewer with these rates screening a pool where 30% would do well gives you hires who are right 70% of the time. Point the same interviewer at an open application pool where only 5% would do well and most of the people they pass are false positives, which is why a cheap early screen is followed by more expensive rounds.

Base rates in reviews

If 30% of a team truly exceeds expectations, a manager with these rates is right about 70% of their “exceeds” ratings. In a group where only 5% truly do, the same manager's “exceeds” is wrong four times in five, which is one reason organisations calibrate against a distribution rather than trusting each manager's rate.

Base rates in wine

In a strong regional flight where 30% of wines merit a medal by the reference panel, a judge with these rates is right about 70% of their medals. In an open competition where 5% merit one, most medals from the same judge go to wines the panel would not medal.

Base rates in screening

This is the screening problem in miniature. A reader who is good on an enriched study set with 30% cancers sees precision collapse in a screening population, where cancer is far rarer than 5%: most recalls are false alarms, which is accepted as the price of the catches, and is why recalls lead to further imaging rather than straight to treatment.

Base rates behind the plate

On a set of borderline pitches where 30% are in the zone, an umpire with these rates is right on 70% of strike calls. On pitches far outside the zone, where 5% are strikes, most strike calls from the same umpire are wrong, so the mix of pitches matters as much as the umpire when comparing call accuracy.

The ≥ 4 row is exactly the confusion matrix of the previous sections: TP 48, FP 21. That cut maximises J, so it is the Youden-optimal threshold. Connecting the six points from (0, 0) to (1, 1) gives the empirical ROC curve.

The curve is a staircase of straight segments because the score has only five levels. Ties at a score level are what make the segments diagonal rather than steps: items with the same score are either all YES or all NO under any threshold, so there is no way to split them, and the straight line is the average of breaking the tie either way.

AUC: the trapezoids, and the Mann–Whitney reading

The area under the ROC curve sums the trapezoids between consecutive points:

AUC = Σ (FPRk+1 − FPRk) · (TPRk + TPRk+1) / 2
Worked · five trapezoids

(0 → 0.05): 0.05 × (0 + 0.4667) / 2 = 0.0117

(0.05 → 0.15): 0.10 × (0.4667 + 0.80) / 2 = 0.0633

(0.15 → 0.3714): 0.2214 × (0.80 + 0.90) / 2 = 0.1882

(0.3714 → 0.6429): 0.2714 × (0.90 + 0.9667) / 2 = 0.2533

(0.6429 → 1): 0.3571 × (0.9667 + 1) / 2 = 0.3512

Sum = 0.8677, exactly 7,289 / 8,400, so AUC ≈ 0.868.

Hanley and McNeil's central point is that this area is a probability: the chance that a randomly chosen truly-YES item gets a higher score than a randomly chosen truly-NO item, counting a tie as half (Hanley and McNeil 1982). That is the quantity the Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney statistic estimates. You can check it by counting pairs: there are 60 × 140 = 8,400 (YES, NO) pairs.

Worked · counting pairs

For each YES score, count NO items strictly below it (wins) and at the same score (ties):

score 1: 2 YES × (0 below, 50 tied) → 0 wins, 100 ties

score 2: 4 × (50 below, 38 tied) → 200 wins, 152 ties

score 3: 6 × (88 below, 31 tied) → 528 wins, 186 ties

score 4: 20 × (119 below, 14 tied) → 2,380 wins, 280 ties

score 5: 28 × (133 below, 7 tied) → 3,724 wins, 196 ties

Wins = 6,832; ties = 914. AUC = (6,832 + 914 / 2) / 8,400 = 7,289 / 8,400 = 0.8677. Same number, to the last digit.

The pair reading is the one to keep in your head. AUC 0.868 means: show the judge one truly-YES and one truly-NO item, and it ranks them the right way round about 87% of the time. AUC is threshold-free, which is both its strength (it measures ranking quality before any cut is chosen) and its weakness (no one deploys a ranking; they deploy a cut, and two judges with the same AUC can have very different confusion matrices at the cut that matters). It is also blind to calibration: multiplying every score by ten changes nothing.

Hanley and McNeil also gave a closed-form standard error, using Q1 = A/(2 − A) and Q2 = 2A²/(1 + A) (form as given in GraphPad's calculation notes):

SE(A) = √[ (A(1 − A) + (n1 − 1)(Q1 − A²) + (n2 − 1)(Q2 − A²)) / (n1 n2) ]
Worked

A = 0.8677, n1 = 60 YES, n2 = 140 NO. Q1 = 0.8677 / 1.1323 = 0.7664. Q2 = 2 × 0.7530 / 1.8677 = 0.8063.

Numerator = 0.1148 + 59 × (0.7664 − 0.7530) + 139 × (0.8063 − 0.7530) = 0.1148 + 0.791 + 7.412 ≈ 8.32. SE = √(8.32 / 8,400) = 0.031.

Approximate 95% interval: 0.868 ± 1.96 × 0.031 = 0.81 to 0.93. (This SE is not in the shared casebook; it is computed for this page.)

Confidence intervals: Wilson, kappa's standard error, the bootstrap

Every number above came from a finite sample of items. A different 60 truly-YES items would have given a different sensitivity. An interval says how far the number could plausibly move. The textbook “Wald” interval, p̂ ± z√(p̂(1 − p̂)/n), behaves badly: Brown, Cai and DasGupta showed its coverage is erratic even at large n and recommend the Wilson interval (or Jeffreys) for small samples (Brown et al. 2001). The Wilson score interval (Wilson 1927) inverts the score test instead:

centre = (p̂ + z²/2n) / (1 + z²/n)    half-width = z √(p̂(1 − p̂)/n + z²/4n²) / (1 + z²/n)    z = 1.96 for 95%
Worked · sensitivity 48/60

p̂ = 0.80, n = 60, z² = 3.8416. Denominator 1 + 3.8416/60 = 1.06403.

Centre = (0.80 + 3.8416/120) / 1.06403 = (0.80 + 0.03201) / 1.06403 = 0.78195.

Inside the root: 0.80 × 0.20 / 60 + 3.8416 / 14,400 = 0.0026667 + 0.0002668 = 0.0029334; root 0.054161. Half-width = 1.96 × 0.054161 / 1.06403 = 0.09977.

Interval 0.78195 ± 0.09977 = 0.682 to 0.882. (Wald would give 0.699 to 0.901, centred on 0.80; Wilson pulls the centre toward 0.5.) Specificity 119/140 by the same steps: 0.782 to 0.900.

Worked · two-judge agreement 75/100

p̂ = 0.75, n = 100. Denominator 1.038416. Centre = (0.75 + 0.019208) / 1.038416 = 0.74075.

Root of 0.001875 + 0.00009604 = 0.0019710 → 0.044397. Half-width = 1.96 × 0.044397 / 1.038416 = 0.08380.

Interval = 0.657 to 0.825, so “0.66–0.83”.

Kappa's standard error. A common large-sample approximation, going back to Cohen's paper and written out in McHugh 2012, treats the chance term as fixed:

SE(κ) ≈ √( po(1 − po) / (n (1 − pe)²) )    95% CI: κ ± 1.96 · SE
Worked · casebook kappa 0.49

po(1 − po) = 0.75 × 0.25 = 0.1875. (1 − pe)² = 0.49² = 0.2401. SE = √(0.1875 / 24.01) = √0.0078093 = 0.0884.

Interval = 0.4898 ± 1.96 × 0.0884 = 0.4898 ± 0.1732 = 0.32 to 0.66.

That formula ignores the sampling noise in pe; Fleiss, Cohen and Everitt derived the fuller large-sample variance (Fleiss, Cohen and Everitt 1969). The approach that needs no formula at all is the bootstrap (Efron 1979): resample the 100 items with replacement, recompute kappa, repeat thousands of times, and read off the middle 95% of the results.

Worked · bootstrap

The casebook script draws 5,000 resamples of the 100 (A, B) pairs (random seed 1), computes kappa on each, sorts them, and takes the 126th and 4,875th values: 0.31 to 0.66. It agrees with the formula to within 0.01 here, because n = 100 and kappa is mid-range. Near 0 or 1, or with rare categories, the two diverge and the bootstrap is the one to trust.

Why this matters: the same two judges, measured on another 100 items, could plausibly have produced a kappa anywhere from about 0.32 (“fair”) to 0.66 (“substantial”). Two judges whose kappas are 0.49 and 0.58 on 100 items each are not distinguishable. A judge-vs-human comparison reported without an interval cannot be acted on, which is the point of Igor's error bars step.

Intervals in hiring

“Ana's hires do well 80% of the time” on 60 hires means somewhere between 68% and 88%. Comparing her with an interviewer at 75% on a similar sample says nothing; the intervals overlap almost entirely.

Intervals in calibration

A calibration exercise on 100 employees that reports manager agreement of 75% is really reporting 66% to 83%. A change to the rating definitions has to move agreement by more than that to show up at all.

Intervals at a competition

A judge who agrees with the reference panel on 75 of 100 wines could plausibly be a 66% judge or an 83% judge. Replicate-pour studies need many wines per judge before they can tell one judge from another.

Intervals in reader studies

A reader who catches 48 of 60 cancers has sensitivity somewhere between 68% and 88%. This is why reader studies use enriched case sets: you need enough cancers in the sample to make the sensitivity interval narrow, and screening populations have very few.

Intervals behind the plate

An umpire right on 75 of 100 borderline pitches is somewhere between 66% and 83%. Pitch tracking settles this by volume: an umpire calls thousands of pitches a season, which shrinks the interval to a point or two.

Sample size: how many items to label

Turn the interval around: pick the half-width h you can live with, and solve for n. For a proportion, using the normal approximation (Wilson gives nearly the same answer once n is in the hundreds):

n ≈ z² p(1 − p) / h²
Worked · a proportion near 0.8

±0.10: 3.8416 × 0.16 / 0.01 = 61.5, so about 62 items (the casebook's 60 YES items give roughly this).

±0.05: 3.8416 × 0.16 / 0.0025 = 246. ±0.03: 683.

Worst case, p = 0.5, ±0.05: 3.8416 × 0.25 / 0.0025 = 385.

For a sensitivity, n counts only the truly-YES items. At 5% prevalence, 246 YES items means about 4,900 items labelled.

For kappa, solve the approximate SE from the previous section for n, using guesses for po and pe (take them from a pilot):

n ≈ z² po(1 − po) / (h² (1 − pe)²)
Worked · casebook-like judges (po = 0.75, pe = 0.51)

±0.17 (what the casebook has): 3.8416 × 0.1875 / (0.0289 × 0.2401) = 104, about the 100 items it used.

±0.10: 0.72030 / (0.01 × 0.2401) = 300. ±0.05: 1,200.

This is rough: it inherits the approximate SE, and with rare YES (large pe) the factor 1/(1 − pe)² blows up, so a rare-event judge needs far more items for the same kappa precision.

The 1/√n intuition. Every interval on this page has n under a square root in its width. Quadrupling the items halves the width: 25 → 100 → 400 → 1,600 items take a proportion near 0.8 from ±0.16 to ±0.078 to ±0.039 to ±0.020. The first hundred labels buy most of the certainty; going from ±0.04 to ±0.02 costs another 1,200. That is the practical shape of eval budgets: label enough to tell apart the decisions you actually face, and stop.

Carry this away. Every judging statistic is a ratio of counts from a small table. Agreement statistics divide by what chance would give (and so swing with prevalence and bias; report ppos and pneg next to kappa). Validity statistics divide by the truth (sensitivity, specificity, J, AUC) or by the verdict (precision, NPV), and only the second kind moves with the base rate. None of them is worth reporting without an interval, and the interval shrinks only as 1/√n.

What you can say now

  • On the casebook, percent agreement 0.75 becomes kappa 0.49 once chance agreement of 0.51 is removed; Scott's pi (0.489) and Krippendorff's alpha (0.491) differ only in how chance is computed.
  • 92% agreement with kappa 0.29 is not a contradiction: rare YES makes chance agreement 0.887, and the identity κ = (PABAK − PI² + BI²)/(1 − PI² + BI²) shows exactly how prevalence and bias move kappa.
  • Fleiss' kappa and Krippendorff's alpha extend this to three judges (0.64 and 0.641); the coincidence matrix makes alpha a two-line calculation and handles missing ratings and ordered scales.
  • Weighted kappa on one 3×3 table can be 0.53, 0.59 or 0.65 depending on the weights, so the weights are part of the policy.
  • Sensitivity 0.80 and specificity 0.85 give J = 0.65 and balanced accuracy 0.825 at any prevalence, but precision is 0.70 at 30% prevalence and 0.22 at 5%.
  • The ROC curve is built by sweeping the threshold over the score table; its trapezoid area, 0.868, equals the probability that a truly-YES item outscores a truly-NO one, checked by counting all 8,400 pairs.
  • Wilson intervals (48/60 → 0.68–0.88; 75/100 → 0.66–0.83) and the kappa interval (0.32–0.66) show how little 100 items pin down, and why each halving of the interval costs four times the labels.
